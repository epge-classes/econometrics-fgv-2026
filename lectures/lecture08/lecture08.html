Introduction

Where We Are

  • We modeled dependence through ARMA models, one variable \(y_t\) at a time
  • Lecture 7: LLNs, CLTs and OLS, all under stationarity
  • Lecture 4 warned us: with \(\phi_1 = 1\), stationarity is gone
  • In reality, several variables interact over time, often with trends
  • Causality here is super hard: no hope for experiments
  • An estimator can never ensure causality. Only careful design can

Today

  • What happens if we regress one non-stationary series on another?
  • How do we tell a unit root from a very persistent series?
  • How do we model the dynamic effect of \(x_t\) on \(y_t\)? AR-DL models
  • Spoiler: nonsense regressions; non-normal limits; long-run multipliers
  • We will sketch the proofs: the full theory is second-year material

Spurious Regressions

A Quick Experiment

  • Let \(u_t\) and \(v_t\) be two independent sequences of i.i.d. \(N(0,1)\) draws
  • Let \(y_t = y_{t-1} + u_t\) and \(x_t = x_{t-1} + v_t\), starting from \(y_0 = x_0 = 0\)
  • Both are random walks, completely independent of each other
  • Both are integrated of order one, \(I(1)\): non-stationary, but the differences are stationary
  • But let’s say you do not know that, and you run

\[ y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + e_t \]

  • What is the truth about \(\beta\)? What do you expect from \(\hat\beta\) and its \(t\)-statistic?

A Monte Carlo Experiment

  • For each \(T\), simulate 2,000 independent pairs of paths \((y_t, x_t)\)
  • In each sample, estimate three regressions by OLS:

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Static:} \quad & y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + e_t \\ \text{Lagged } y\text{:} \quad & y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + \gamma y_{t-1} + e_t \\ \text{Lagged } y \text{ and } x\text{:} \quad & y_t = \alpha + \beta x_t + \delta x_{t-1} + \gamma y_{t-1} + e_t \end{aligned} \]

  • In each one, run the usual \(t\)-test of \(H_0: \beta = 0\) at the 5% level
  • The null is true, so we should reject 5% of the time
  • Would you find stars in your regression table?

Rejection Rates for \(\beta\) at the 5% Level

What Is Going On?

  • In all three regressions, the true \(\beta\) is zero
  • Lagged \(y\) regressions are correctly specified, with \(\gamma = 1\) and \(e_t = u_t\)
  • The static one is not: with \(\beta = 0\), its error is \(y_t - \alpha\), a random walk!
  • And the regressors are not stationary. With \(x_0 = 0\):

\[ \Var(x_t) = \Var\left(\sum_{s=1}^{t} v_s\right) = t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \E\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}x_t^2\right] = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} t = \frac{T+1}{2} \to \infty \]

  • There is no constant \(\symbf{Q}\) for the sample second moments to converge to
  • So the ergodic theorem and the CLTs of Lecture 7 are all off the table

Why the Static \(t\)-Statistic Explodes

Orders of magnitude in the static regression (Phillips, 1986)

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat\beta &= O_p(1) && \text{converges to a random variable, not to zero} \\ \sum_{t=1}^{T}(x_t - \bar x)^2 &= O_p(T^2) && \text{each term is of order } t \\ s^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat e_t^2 &= O_p(T) && \text{the residuals inherit the random walk} \\ \text{se}(\hat\beta) = \frac{s}{\sqrt{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2}} &= O_p\left(T^{-1/2}\right) && \text{a standard error that vanishes...} \\ t = \hat\beta / \text{se}(\hat\beta) &= O_p\left(T^{1/2}\right) && \text{...around a slope that does not} \end{aligned} \]

  • More data makes things worse: rejection rates go to one
  • \(R^2\) does not vanish: median about 0.17 at any \(T\), above 0.5 in 1 of 6 samples
  • Granger and Newbold (1974): at \(T = 50\), 76% rejections and an average \(R^2\) of 0.26

Why Lags Help

  • Lagged \(y\): the error is i.i.d. and \(\hat\beta \xrightarrow{p} 0\), yet the rejection rate gets stuck near 17%
  • \(x_t\) is \(I(1)\), so the \(t\)-statistic has a non-standard limit, even in a correct model
  • Lagged \(y\) and \(x\): rewrite the two terms in \(x\) as

\[ \beta x_t + \delta x_{t-1} = \beta\,\Delta x_t + (\beta + \delta)\,x_{t-1} \]

  • Now \(\beta\) is the coefficient on a stationary, mean-zero regressor
  • Such coefficients have standard limits (Sims, Stock and Watson, 1990)
  • But not all of them: a joint test of \(\beta = \delta = 0\) is still non-standard
  • Regressing \(\Delta y_t\) on \(\Delta x_t\) also fixes the problem: back to Lecture 7

Empirical Takeaway

What to Remember

  • Correlation does not imply causation, especially in time series
  • A static regression of \(I(1)\) on \(I(1)\) can be pure nonsense, whatever \(t\) and \(R^2\) say
  • Dynamic specification or differencing can restore standard inference
  • One context makes static levels regressions meaningful: cointegration
  • Lecture 7 does not apply to non-stationary data: we need new theorems
  • These are one order of magnitude more sophisticated
  • Good stuff for a second-year class, right? 😉

Testing for a Unit Root

Persistent or Unit Root?

  • You want to know whether \(y_t\) is stationary, and you estimate an AR(1):

\[ y_t = \rho y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \iid N(0, \sigma^2), \quad y_0 = 0 \]

  • The OLS estimator is

\[ \hat{\rho} = \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_ty_{t-1}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}^2} = \rho + \frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}^2} \]

  • Let’s say you get \(\hat\rho = 0.96\). A very persistent series, or a non-stationary one?
  • If \(|\rho| < 1\), we know that \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\rho} - \rho) \xrightarrow{d} N\left(0, 1 - \rho^2\right)\)
  • Can we just use this and a \(t\)-test of \(H_0: \rho = 1\)?

The Numerator When \(\rho = 1\)

  • Now \(y_t \sim N(0, \sigma^2 t)\). Squaring \(y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t\) gives \(y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t = \frac{1}{2}\left(y_t^2 - y_{t-1}^2 - \varepsilon_t^2\right)\)
  • Sum over \(t\). The sum telescopes, and \(y_0 = 0\):

\[ \frac{1}{\sigma^2 T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{y_T^2}{\sigma^2 T} - \frac{1}{\sigma^2 T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\varepsilon_t^2\right) \]

  • First term: \(y_T / (\sigma\sqrt{T}) \sim N(0,1)\), so it is a \(\chi^2_1\). Second term: goes to 1 by the LLN
  • By Slutsky:

\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{\sigma^2 T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t \xrightarrow{d} \frac{1}{2}\left(\chi^2_{1} - 1\right)} \]

  • The numerator is \(O_p(T)\), and its limit is not normal

The Denominator and the Right Rate

\[ \E\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}^2\right] = \sigma^2\sum_{t=1}^{T}(t-1) = \sigma^2\,\frac{T(T-1)}{2} \approx \sigma^2\,\frac{T^2}{2} \]

  • The denominator is \(O_p(T^2)\), so the right rate is \(T\), not \(\sqrt{T}\):

\[ T(\hat{\rho} - 1) = \frac{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t}{\frac{1}{T^2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}y_{t-1}^2} \]

  • This explains why \(N(0, 1 - \rho^2)\) collapses to a point at \(\rho = 1\): \(\sqrt{T}\) is too slow
  • The scaled denominator stays random: no LLN here, so no Slutsky
  • To get the limit, we need a tool for the whole path of \(y_t\)

The Dickey-Fuller Distributions

Theorem (Functional CLT)

If \(\rho = 1\), then \(\frac{y_{\lfloor rT \rfloor}}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} \Rightarrow W(r)\) on \([0,1]\), where \(W\) is a standard Brownian motion.

  • So \(\frac{1}{\sigma^2 T^2}\sum_t y_{t-1}^2 \xrightarrow{d} \int_0^1 W(r)^2\,dr\), with the same \(W\) as in \(W(1)^2 \sim \chi^2_1\) above

Theorem (Dickey and Fuller, 1979; Hansen, Thms. 16.9-16.10)

With \(\hat s_T = s\,/\sqrt{\sum_t y_{t-1}^2}\) and \(s^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t\hat\varepsilon_t^2\), if \(\rho = 1\):

\[ T(\hat\rho - 1) \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(W(1)^2 - 1\right)}{\int_0^1 W(r)^2\,dr} \qquad\text{and}\qquad t = \frac{\hat\rho - 1}{\hat s_T} \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\frac{1}{2}\left(W(1)^2 - 1\right)}{\left(\int_0^1 W(r)^2\,dr\right)^{1/2}} \]

  • No unknown parameters: simulate them with any precision you want

The Distributions Are Far From Normal

Back to \(\hat\rho = 0.96\)

  • Fit an AR(1) with a constant, and suppose the truth is a random walk
  • Then \(T(\hat\rho - 1)\) has median around \(-4.4\) and 5% quantile around \(-13.8\)
  • With \(T = 100\): \(T(\hat\rho - 1) = -4\). That’s the median outcome under a unit root!
  • With \(T = 1{,}000\): \(T(\hat\rho - 1) = -40\). A clear rejection
  • OLS is biased downward here: a random walk looks mean-reverting in sample
  • The same \(\hat\rho\) means very different things at different \(T\)

AR(\(p\)) and Unit Roots

  • What if the true process is an AR(\(p\))?

\[ y_t = \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \ldots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t \]

  • \(z = 1\) is a root of the AR polynomial if and only if \(\phi_1 + \ldots + \phi_p = 1\)
  • Let all other roots be outside the unit circle, so \(\Delta y_t\) is stationary
  • Rewrite the model in terms of one level and \(p - 1\) differences (appendix):

\[ y_t = \rho y_{t-1} + \beta_1 \Delta y_{t-1} + \ldots + \beta_{p-1} \Delta y_{t-p+1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \rho = \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i \]

  • Under \(H_0: \rho = 1\) (Hansen, Thm. 16.13): \(\sqrt{T}(\hat\beta - \beta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \symbf{V})\) is standard
  • \(T(\hat\rho - 1)\) goes to \(\left(1 - \sum_j \beta_j\right)\) times the Dickey-Fuller limit: a nuisance parameter

The Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) Test

  • The \(t\)-statistic of \(\hat\rho\) is free of that nuisance parameter:

\[ t_{ADF} \equiv \frac{\hat{\rho} - 1}{\hat{s}_T}, \qquad \hat s_T \text{ is the usual OLS standard error of } \hat\rho \]

  • Same limit as in the AR(1), so same critical values (Hansen, Thm. 16.14)
  • Reject the unit root if \(t_{ADF} < c_\alpha\): only large negative values count
  • Software runs \(\Delta y_t = \pi y_{t-1} + \ldots\) with \(\pi = \rho - 1\). Same \(t\)-statistic
  • Lag length: too few distorts the size, too many costs power
  • Reasonable default: pick \(p\) by AIC. ARMA errors: let \(p\) grow with \(T\) (Said and Dickey, 1984)

Other Tests

  • Phillips and Perron (1988): same hypotheses as the ADF test
  • Run the Dickey-Fuller regression with no extra lags
  • Then correct \(t\) for serial correlation using a long-run variance: Lecture 7!
  • No lag length \(p\) to choose, but a bandwidth \(q\) instead
  • KPSS (1992) flips the hypotheses: \(H_0\): stationary vs. \(H_1\): unit root
  • Regress \(y_t\) on a constant, and maybe a trend. Partial sums of residuals should stay small
  • Scale them by a long-run variance. Reject for large values
  • ADF does not reject and KPSS rejects: evidence for a unit root
  • ADF rejects and KPSS does not: evidence for stationarity
  • Both reject or neither does: the data are not telling you much

Using Unit Root Tests

  • “We cannot reject” does not mean “we found a unit root”
  • “Levels if ADF rejects, differences if not” asks the test a question it was not built for
  • Start from economics: should this series be mean-reverting? Then look at the evidence
  • One-off structural breaks can look like unit roots (Perron, 1989)
  • Think of Brazilian inflation before and after 1994
  • Also look at \(\hat\rho\) and its magnitude, not only at the \(p\)-value (Hansen, Sec. 16.13)

The Bayesian Critique

  • As a function of \(\rho\), the Gaussian likelihood given \(y_0\) (and \(\sigma^2\)) is always the same shape:

\[ L(\rho) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{\sum_t y_{t-1}^2}{2\sigma^2}\left(\rho - \hat\rho\right)^2\right) \]

  • With a flat prior, \(\rho \mid \text{data} \sim N\left(\hat\rho,\ \sigma^2 / \sum_t y_{t-1}^2\right)\): symmetric, and nothing special at \(\rho = 1\)
  • Frequentist object: \(\hat\rho\) given \(\rho\). Skewed, and the theory jumps at \(\rho = 1\)
  • Bayesian object: \(\rho\) given \(\hat\rho\). The same joint distribution, cut the other way
  • Very cool paper: Understanding Unit Rooters: A Helicopter Tour (Sims and Uhlig, 1991)
  • You need no \(\rho = 1\) vs. \(\rho < 1\) decision, but the prior now matters
  • Super popular among the empirical macro and DSGE crowd! Second-year stuff, again

AR-DL Models

An Example of Monetary Policy Adjustment

  • Let \(i_t\) be the nominal interest rate set by the central bank at time \(t\)
  • Let \(\pi_t^e\) be the inflation expectation at \(t\) for \(t+1\)
  • Let \(r_t^* = \alpha + \symbf{x}_t'\symbf{\beta}\) be an equilibrium real rate
  • \(\symbf{x}_t\): productivity growth, unemployment, foreign interest rates, risk premia, etc
  • Let \(i_t^* = r_t^* + \pi_t^e\) be the “desired” nominal interest rate
  • At time \(t\), assume that the central bank dislikes missing \(i_t^*\) and moving the rate:

\[ \min_{i_t} \ \left(i_t - i_t^*\right)^2 + \theta \left(i_t - i_{t-1}\right)^2 \]

Can We Estimate This Rule?

  • The solution is a weighted average of the target and the past rate:

\[ i_t = \frac{1}{1+\theta}\,i_t^* + \frac{\theta}{1+\theta}\,i_{t-1} = \frac{1}{1+\theta}\left(\alpha + \symbf{x}_t'\symbf{\beta} + \pi_t^e\right) + \frac{\theta}{1+\theta}\,i_{t-1} \]

  • Add a policy shock \(\varepsilon_t\) and run:

\[ i_t = a_0 + a_1 i_{t-1} + \symbf{x}_t'\symbf{\delta} + \gamma \pi_t^e + \varepsilon_t \]

  • Then \(\theta = a_1 / (1 - a_1)\), and the model implies \(a_1 + \gamma = 1\)
  • Exercise: How would you test this restriction? With which standard errors?
  • Under what conditions on \((\symbf{x}_t, \pi_t^e)\) and \(\varepsilon_t\) is OLS consistent here?
  • What if \(i_t\) also moves \(\symbf{x}_t\)? And \(\pi_t^e\)? How do we interpret the coefficients then?

The AR-DL Model

  • You just saw an AR-DL(\(p,q\)), also known as ADL(\(p,q\)):

\[ y_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\,y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{q}\symbf{\beta}_j'\,\symbf{x}_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t \]

  • With \(p = 0\): a distributed lag model, DL(\(q\))
  • Estimate by OLS. If \((y_t, \symbf{x}_t)\) is stationary, Lecture 7 applies
  • Useful for forecasting. On its own, it is silent about causality
  • Enough lags, so \(\varepsilon_t\) is unforecastable: scores are an MDS. Use White
  • DL model or too few lags: \(\varepsilon_t\) is autocorrelated. Use Newey-West
  • With \(y_{t-1}\) on the right, autocorrelated \(\varepsilon_t\) means \(\E[y_{t-1}\varepsilon_t] \neq 0\): only a projection survives

Interpreting the Coefficients

  • Take a scalar \(x_t\) and a DL(\(q\)): a change in \(x_t\) moves \(y_t, y_{t+1}, \ldots, y_{t+q}\) directly
  • \(\beta_0\): impact effect. \(\beta_0 + \ldots + \beta_h\): cumulative effect after \(h\) periods
  • \(\beta_0 + \ldots + \beta_q\): the long-run multiplier
  • With lags of \(y_t\), effects also propagate through the AR part. Set \(y_t = y^*\) and \(x_t = x^*\):

\[ y^* = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\,y^* + \sum_{j=0}^{q}\beta_j\,x^* \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\frac{\partial y^*}{\partial x^*} = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^{q}\beta_j}{1 - \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i}} \]

  • Standard error: delta method. Testing whether it is zero: just test \(\sum_j \beta_j = 0\)
  • Careful: \(x_t\) may also induce changes in \(x_{t+1}, x_{t+2}\), etc
  • Without a full model, read the \(\beta_j\)’s as partial correlations

Illustration: Exchange Rate Pass-Through

  • \(\pi_t\): IPCA inflation. \(\Delta s_t\): depreciation. The ADF test rejects a unit root in both

\[ \pi_t = \alpha + \phi_1\pi_{t-1} + \sum_{j=1}^{6}\beta_j\,\Delta s_{t-j} + \varepsilon_t \]

  • A one-off 1% depreciation raises the price level by about 0.09% in the long run
  • Is this a causal pass-through? What else moves the exchange rate and prices together?

Can We Ever Hope to Get to Causality?

  • To get to causality, we typically need:
    • Exogenous variation in \(x_t\)
    • An explicit link of how \(x_t\) affects \(y_t\) over time
  • There is a huge literature on this, and what people run is a VAR
  • A VAR(\(p\)) is a system of AR-DL models treating \(K\) variables symmetrically:

\[ \symbf{y}_t = \symbf{\alpha} + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\symbf{\Phi}_i\,\symbf{y}_{t-i} + \symbf{\varepsilon}_t, \qquad \symbf{y}_t \in \R^K, \quad \symbf{\Phi}_i \in \R^{K \times K} \]

  • Assumptions on shocks and parameters can deliver causal interpretations
  • Out of scope here, but very good stuff for a second-year class! 👀

Takeaways

What to Remember

  • Regressing \(I(1)\) on \(I(1)\) can produce nonsense: static \(t\)-statistics diverge
  • Under a unit root, the rate is \(T\) and limits are non-normal: use ADF critical values
  • Unit root tests have low power: failing to reject is not finding a unit root
  • AR-DL models: read long-run multipliers, and check whether \(\varepsilon_t\) is an MDS

Appendix

Proof: Rewriting an AR(\(p\))

  • Let \(\symbf{y}_{t-1} = (y_{t-1}, \ldots, y_{t-p})'\) and consider the invertible \(p \times p\) matrix

\[ \symbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 1 & -1 & 0 & \cdots & 0\\ 0 & 1 & -1 & \cdots & 0\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \symbf{B}\symbf{y}_{t-1} = \begin{bmatrix} y_{t-1} \\ \Delta y_{t-1} \\ \vdots \\ \Delta y_{t-p+1} \end{bmatrix} \]

  • Define \((\rho, \symbf{\beta}')'\) through \(\symbf{\phi} = \symbf{B}'(\rho, \symbf{\beta}')'\). Then \(\symbf{\phi}'\symbf{y}_{t-1} = (\rho, \symbf{\beta}')\symbf{B}\symbf{y}_{t-1}\), which is the rewritten model
  • Reading off the rows: \(\phi_1 = \rho + \beta_1\), \(\phi_i = \beta_i - \beta_{i-1}\) for \(1 < i < p\), and \(\phi_p = -\beta_{p-1}\)
  • Summing them, the \(\beta\)’s telescope: \(\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i = \rho\)
  • Exercise: Show that \(\beta_j = -\sum_{i=j+1}^{p}\phi_i\).
