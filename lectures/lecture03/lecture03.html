Introduction

Types of Data

Common data structures in Economics:

  • Cross-sectional data: many units, one time period
    • Grades of several students in one exam, GDP growth of several countries in a given year
  • Time series data: one unit, many time periods
    • Inflation over time in one country, rainfall in a given area, the price of a stock
  • Panel data: many units, many time periods for the same units
    • GDP growth of several countries over several years, prices of several products over time
  • Text, spatial data, images, etc.

Where We Are

  • So far: mostly cross-sectional methods (everything about \(Y_i\))
  • Next step: time series methods (everything about \(Y_t\))
  • Next next step: panel data methods (everything about \(Y_{i,t}\)), probably the most prevalent type nowadays
  • Next weeks: focus on understanding the challenges of time series data
  • This lecture: how is a time series different from cross-sectional data? \(+\) important definitions
  • Main reference: Time Series Analysis, by James Hamilton
  • Hansen’s book provides a nice introduction, but it is too short on the topic

What Is a Time Series?

  • A time series is a sequence of observations on a variable (or several variables) over time, at equally spaced intervals
  • Example: annual population of a country
  • Unlike cross-sectional data, time series data is ordered
  • In this course, time is discrete: we observe data at specific intervals (days, months, years…)
  • Continuous-time series (e.g., high-frequency financial data) are a more advanced topic, with a vast literature as well

Why Should You Care?

  1. Policy evaluation: every policy takes place over time
    • What’s the impact of a reform? Before vs after? Long-lasting effects? Fast die-outs?
    • A very important building block for panel data methods
  2. Forecasting: what could the future look like?
    • What will inflation be next month? How many students will enroll next semester?
    • How much inventory should a firm hold? What’s the likely path of deforestation in a given area?
  3. Nowcasting: high(er)-frequency monitoring of low-frequency variables
    • What is the current state of the economy? How many people are unemployed right now?
  1. It has very elegant math behind it!

The Role of Dependence

  • In cross-sectional data, we typically assume that observations are i.i.d.
  • If we relabel observations, we have no problems
  • In time series, independence is a rare exception
  • Their distribution might also change over time
  • If we relabel observations, we change the meaning of the data

Examples:

  • If inflation is high this month, it will likely be high next month
  • If a student is doing well on every exam, they will likely do well on the next one
  • The major difference w.r.t. cross-sectional data: the future might depend on the past

Building Blocks

Random Variables

Formally, the right way to think about a time series is as a stochastic process. First, what is a random variable?

Definition (Random Variable)

Given a probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal{F},\Pr)\), a random variable is a real function \(Y:\Omega\rightarrow\R\) such that \(\{\omega\in\Omega : Y(\omega)\leq c\}\in\mathcal{F}\) for all \(c\in \R\).

  • \(\Omega\) is a sample space. Example: the numbers on a die (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)
  • \(\mathcal{F}\) is a collection of events. Example: even numbers
  • \(\Pr: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]\) is a probability measure. Example: probability of rolling an even number
  • Think about an i.i.d. sample as different realizations of \(\omega\): \(Y_1 = Y(\omega_1), Y_2 = Y(\omega_2), \dots\)

Stochastic Processes

Definition (Stochastic Process, a Bit Informal)

A stochastic process is an ordered family of random variables \(\{Y_t, t \in \mathcal{T}\}\) defined on a probability space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \Pr)\), where \(\mathcal{T}\) is an ordered set. For a fixed \(\omega \in \Omega\), the path \(\{Y_t(\omega), t \in \mathcal{T}\}\) is one realization of the process.

Loosely speaking:

  • A random variable is a way to model an uncertain number
  • A stochastic process is a way to model an uncertain path
  • In reality, we observe one, and only one, realization of the stochastic process
  • Think about the history of the world: it is a single realization from \(\Omega\)
  • YOLO: you only live once!

YOLO

The Challenge Ahead

  • You only observe the red path, but the blue ones were equally likely to have happened
  • The main challenge: you see one path and want to make inference about the whole process

Example: inflation in Brazil in 1989

  • We know it was super high
  • But we have only one reading of inflation for 1989
  • When can we say something about the “inflation process” in Brazil given only one observation?

We will need to impose a lot of structure! Without structure, we are lost!

Stationarity and Ergodicity

Weak Stationarity

Definition (Weak Stationarity)

A stochastic process \(\{Y_t\}\) is weakly stationary (or second-order stationary, or covariance stationary) if, and only if, its first two unconditional population moments exist and are constant over time: \[ \begin{aligned} &\E[Y_t]=\mu, \quad |\mu|<\infty, \quad \forall\, t\in\mathcal{T}\\ &\E[(Y_t-\mu)(Y_{t-h}-\mu)]=\gamma_h, \quad |\gamma_h|<\infty, \quad \forall\, t\in\mathcal{T}, \quad h=0,\pm 1,\pm 2, \ldots \end{aligned} \]

True or false?

  • \(\gamma_0 = \Var(Y_t)\)
  • \(\gamma_h > \gamma_{-h}\) for \(h \neq 0\)
  • An i.i.d. process with finite variance is weakly stationary

What Process Has a Higher \(\gamma_1\)?

Dial the Persistence

\(Y_t = \rho\, Y_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\varepsilon_t\) with \(\varepsilon_t \iid N(0,1)\). The shocks stay fixed: only \(\rho\) changes

  return Array.from(y, (v, t) => ({t, y: v}))
}

Strong (or Strict) Stationarity

Definition (Strong Stationarity)

A stochastic process \(\{Y_t\}\) is strongly stationary (or strictly stationary) if, and only if, its joint distributions are invariant to time shifts: for every \(n\), every \(t_1, \dots, t_n\) and every \(\tau\), \[ F_{t_1,\ldots,t_n}(y_1,\ldots,y_n)=F_{t_1+\tau,\ldots,t_n+\tau}(y_1,\ldots,y_n) \quad \forall\, (y_1, \ldots, y_n) \] where \(F_{t_1,\ldots,t_n}\) is the joint CDF of \((Y_{t_1},\ldots,Y_{t_n})\).

True or false?

  • If a process is weakly stationary, it is also strongly stationary
  • If a process is strongly stationary, it is also weakly stationary
  • An i.i.d. process with finite variance is strongly stationary

Ergodicity: Motivation

  • Let’s call the mysterious process from the YOLO simulation \(m_t\)
  • Denote by \(m_t^{(i)}\) the realization of \(m\) at time \(t\) for the \(i\)-th path
  • The paths are independent (because I chose so!)
  • Let’s say I want to estimate two (potentially different) quantities: \(\E[m_{20}]\) and \(\E[m_{80}]\)
  • How can I do that?

Ergodicity: Motivation

Ergodicity: Motivation

Proposal 1: estimate \(\E[m_{20}]\) by averaging the values at \(t=20\) across all paths

  • Formally: \(\hat{m}_{20} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_{20}^{(i)}\), where \(n\) is the number of paths. Same for \(t=80\)
  • Would this yield a consistent estimator?
  • What’s the main problem with this approach?

Proposal 2: I do not have access to the whole process, so I use the one path I have

  • Estimate \(\E[m_{20}]\) by averaging across all time periods in the path: \(\widetilde{m}_{20} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} m_{t}^{(1)}\)
  • Would I need stationarity for this to yield a consistent estimator?
  • Intuitively, would that be enough?

Ergodicity: Definition

  • Ergodicity is a property that some stochastic processes have
  • Intuitive definition (don’t quote me on this):

Definition (Ergodicity, Intuitive)

A stochastic process \(\{Y_t\}\) is ergodic if its realized paths are “rich enough” with probability 1. By “rich enough”, we mean that, with probability 1, it will neither get stuck in a subset of the state space nor fall into cyclic trajectories.

Said another way: if you observe a process for a long enough \(T\), you will be able to learn everything about the process

Ergodicity vs Stationarity

  • There are ways to test whether a process is stationary. We will get there
  • There is no way to test for ergodicity! You have to assume it!

Important:

  • When a process is stationary, there are simple conditions that imply ergodicity
  • Under ergodicity and strict stationarity, \(\widetilde{m}_{20}\) is consistent!
  • This result is called the Ergodic Theorem (Theorem 14.9 in Hansen’s book)

Also important:

  • Stationarity does not imply ergodicity (next slide)
  • Exercise (problem set): show that a stationary process is not ergodic

Time Averages vs. Ensemble Averages

Two stationary processes, 50 paths each:

  • Ergodic: \(Y_t = \varepsilon_t\), \(\varepsilon_t \iid N(0,1)\)
  • Not ergodic: \(Y_t = X_t^2\), \(X_t = S\,\varepsilon_t\), \(S^2 \in \{0.5, 1.5\}\) drawn once per path (50/50)
viewof Tmax = Inputs.range([10, 5000], {value: 1000, step: 1, transform: Math.log, label: "Sample size T"})
ergo = {
  const rng = d3.randomLcg(126)
  const draw = d3.randomNormal.source(rng)(0, 1)
  const T = 5000
  const grid = Array.from(new Set(d3.range(301).map(j => Math.round(Math.exp(j * Math.log(T) / 300)))))
  return d3.range(50).map(i => {
    const S2 = rng() < 0.5 ? 0.5 : 1.5
    let sum1 = 0, sum2 = 0, k = 0
    const avgs = []
    for (let t = 1; t <= T; t++) {
      const e = draw()
      sum1 += e
      sum2 += e * e
      if (t === grid[k]) { avgs.push({t, m1: sum1 / t, m2: sum2 / t}); k++ }
    }
    return {path: i, S2, avgs}
  })
}

// Left: Y = e, so its time average is that of e. Right: Y = X^2 = S^2 e^2, so its time average is S^2 times that of e^2
ergoRows = ergo.flatMap(p => p.avgs
  .filter(d => d.t <= Tmax)
  .map(d => ({path: p.path, t: d.t, m1: d.m1, m2: p.S2 * d.m2})))

Formal Definition: Ergodicity (Time Allowing)

Trivial Invariant Events

  • Let \(G \subset \R^{\infty}\) and let \(\tilde{Y}_t = (\dots, Y_{t-1}, Y_t, Y_{t+1}, \dots)\) be the history of the process
  • An event \(A\) has the form \(A = \{\omega \in \Omega : \tilde{Y}_t \in G\}\)
  • The \(l\)-th time shift of \(A\) is \(A_l = \{\omega \in \Omega : \tilde{Y}_{t+l} \in G\}\)
  • An event \(A\) is called invariant if \(A_l = A\) for all \(l \in \mathbb{Z}\)
  • An event \(A\) is called trivial if \(\Pr(A) = 0\) or \(\Pr(A) = 1\)

Definition (Ergodicity)

A process \(Y_t\) is ergodic if every invariant event is trivial.

Example

  • Consider the event \(A = \{\sup_{t \in \mathbb{Z}} Y_t \leq 0\}\)
  • In words: the process never takes a positive value
  • For any \(l\), \(A_l = \{\sup_{t \in \mathbb{Z}} Y_{t+l} \leq 0\}\)
  • Is this event invariant?
  • Suppose \(Y_t = Z\) for all \(t \in \mathbb{Z}\), where \(Z \sim U[-1, 1]\)
  • This means that the process is constant after \(Z\) is drawn
  • What’s \(\Pr(A)\) here?
  • Is this process ergodic?

One Useful Characterization of Ergodicity

Theorem (Ergodicity, Characterization)

A strictly stationary series \(Y_t \in \R^{m}\) is ergodic if, and only if, for all events \(A\) and \(B\) \[ \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{l=1}^{n} \Pr(A_l \cap B) = \Pr(A) \cdot \Pr(B) \]

  • Intuition: as we go back in time, on average, events become nearly independent

Two Useful Theorems

Stationarity + Ergodicity = LLN

  • You will be interested in approximating means and second moments of a process
  • Stationarity + ergodicity is the “right set of assumptions” to do that

Theorem (Hannan, 1970, Theorem 2, p. 203)

If \(Y_t\) is strictly stationary and ergodic with \(\E|Y_t| < \infty\), then \[ \bar{Y}_T = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} Y_t \xrightarrow{a.s.} \E[Y_t] \quad \text{as} \quad T \rightarrow \infty \] If, also, \(\E[Y_t^2] < \infty\), then \[ \hat{\gamma}_h = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T-h} (Y_t - \bar{Y}_T)(Y_{t+h} - \bar{Y}_T) \xrightarrow{a.s.} \gamma_h \quad \text{as} \quad T \rightarrow \infty \]

What to Do in Practice?

  • The last theorem assumes something we cannot test. If we only care about estimation, there is an easier way out:

Theorem (Hannan, 1970, Theorem 6, p. 210)

If \(Y_t\) is weakly stationary and \(\sum_{h=1}^{\infty} |\gamma_h| < \infty\), then \[ \bar{Y}_T = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} Y_t \xrightarrow{L_2} \E[Y_t] \quad \text{as} \quad T \rightarrow \infty \] If, in addition, \(\E[Y_t^4] < \infty\) and the fourth-order cumulants of \(Y_t\) are absolutely summable, then \(\hat{\gamma}_h \xrightarrow{L_2} \gamma_h\) as \(T \rightarrow \infty\).

Check the fourth-moment condition for \(\hat{\gamma}_h\) against Hannan (1970), p. 210

  • Recall: \(L_2\) convergence implies convergence in probability, but it neither implies nor is implied by a.s. convergence

White Noise and Martingale Difference Sequences

White Noise

  • In the cross-section, it’s common to write \(y_i = \alpha + \beta x_i + u_i\) with \(u_i\) i.i.d.
  • Now we need weaker notions of “error term”: white noise processes and martingale difference sequences

Definition (White Noise Process)

A sequence of random variables \((\varepsilon_t)_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}\) is a white noise process if:

  1. \(\E[\varepsilon_t] = 0\) for all \(t\)
  2. \(\Var(\varepsilon_t) = \sigma^2 < \infty\) for all \(t\)
  3. \(\Cov(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_s) = 0\) for all \(t \neq s\)

In words: uncorrelated random variables with mean zero and constant variance.

Any i.i.d. sequence of variables with mean zero and finite variance is a white noise process.

Information Sets

  • Conditioning on past information is common in economic models and in Econometrics
  • The correct mathematical objects here are \(\sigma\)-fields and filtrations. We will avoid the measure-theoretic formalism for now
  • Intuitively: the \(\sigma\)-field (or \(\sigma\)-algebra) generated by \((Y_t, Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2}, \dots)\) is the collection of possible histories of this process up to \(t\)
  • We write \(\mathcal{F}_t = \sigma(Y_t, Y_{t-1}, Y_{t-2}, \dots)\): all information available up to time \(t\)
  • In other areas of Economics, we typically call \(\sigma\)-fields information sets
  • Example: \(\E[Y_t | \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]\) is our best guess for \(Y_t\) given information from this process, and only that, up to \(t-1\)

Martingale Difference Sequences

Definition (Martingale Difference Sequence)

A sequence of random variables \((\varepsilon_t)_{t \in \mathbb{Z}}\) is a martingale difference sequence (MDS) with respect to the filtration \(\{\mathcal{F}_t\}\) if:

  1. \(\varepsilon_t\) is adapted to \(\mathcal{F}_t\)
  2. \(\E|\varepsilon_t| < \infty\) for all \(t\)
  3. \(\E[\varepsilon_t | \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0\) almost surely for all \(t\)

True or false?

  • Every MDS has (unconditional) mean zero
  • \(\Cov(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t+h}) = 0\) for all \(h \neq 0\)
  • \(\E[\varepsilon_{t+h} | \mathcal{F}_{t}] = 0\) for all \(h > 0\)
  • \(\E[\varepsilon_{t-h} | \mathcal{F}_{t}] = 0\) for all \(h > 0\)

Example

  • Let \(u_t\) be an i.i.d. sequence with mean zero and variance \(\sigma^2 < \infty\)
  • Define \(v_t = u_t \cdot u_{t-1}\)
    • Is this process i.i.d.? Is it an MDS with respect to its natural filtration? Is it white noise?
  • Now define \(m_t = u_t + u_{t-1}\cdot u_{t-2}\)
    • Is this process i.i.d.? Is it an MDS with respect to its natural filtration? Is it white noise?
