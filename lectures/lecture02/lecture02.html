Motivation

The Margin of Error You See on TV

  • An online poll asks \(n\) voters whether they will vote for candidate A
  • Online polls are self-selected, but let’s pretend respondents are iid today ;)
  • \(X_i = 1\) if respondent \(i\) picks A, so \(p = P\{X_i = 1\}\) and \(\hat p = \bar X_n\)
  • The CLT and Slutsky’s theorem give \[ \frac{\sqrt n\,(\hat p - p)}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1) \quad\implies\quad \hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}} \]
  • In our poll, \(n = 2{,}000\) and \(\hat p =\) 37.8%: the “margem de erro” is \(\pm\) 2.1 p.p.
  • Two ingredients: a known limit, and a nuisance parameter we can estimate

Now the Whole TV Graphic

Each respondent also declares a state. Here are 18 95% intervals from the same poll:

  • Each interval is right 95% of the time. What’s the chance that all 18 are right?
  • Simulating many polls from the truth: only about 42%. The red ones missed

A Joint Confidence Set

  • \(p_{s,k}\): share of candidate \(k \in \{A, B, C\}\) among voters in state \(s\)
  • \(N_s\): number of respondents from state \(s\), and \(\widehat{se}_{s,k} = \sqrt{\hat p_{s,k}(1-\hat p_{s,k})/N_s}\)
  • We want one statement about all 18 shares at once: \[ P\left\{ p_{s,k} \in \left[\hat p_{s,k} - c\cdot \widehat{se}_{s,k},\ \hat p_{s,k} + c\cdot \widehat{se}_{s,k}\right] \text{ for every } s, k \right\} = 1 - \alpha \]
  • Same form as on TV: only the multiplier \(c\) changes
  • \(c = 1.96\) is too small. How large must \(c\) be?

Why This Root?

Let \(t_{s,k} \equiv (\hat p_{s,k} - p_{s,k})/\widehat{se}_{s,k}\), the usual t-statistic. Then \[ \text{all 18 intervals cover} \iff |t_{s,k}| \le c \ \text{ for every } s,k \iff \underbrace{\max_{s,k}\, |t_{s,k}|}_{\equiv\, R_n} \le c \]

  • So \(c\) is the \(1-\alpha\) quantile of \(R_n\): the whole problem boils down to one number
  • Why divide by \(\widehat{se}_{s,k}\)? Shares in RS are much noisier than in SP
  • Without it, every interval gets the same width: \(\hat p_{s,k} \pm c\)
  • Then the noisy RS cells set \(c\), and SP gets needlessly wide intervals
  • Studentizing puts all 18 on the same scale: each one gets a fair share of \(\alpha\)

What Is the Distribution of \(R_n\)?

  • CLT + delta method: \((t_{s,k})_{s,k} \xrightarrow{d} Z \sim N(0, \Sigma(P))\), so \(R_n \xrightarrow{d} \max_{s,k}|Z_{s,k}|\)
  • Across states, the \(Z\)’s are independent. Within a state, candidates compete for voters: \[ \Corr(Z_{s,j}, Z_{s,k}) = -\sqrt{\frac{p_{s,j}\, p_{s,k}}{(1-p_{s,j})(1-p_{s,k})}}, \qquad j \neq k \]
  • The maximum of 18 correlated normals has no closed-form quantile
  • And that quantile depends on the unknown shares \(p_{s,k}\)

What we really want

The distribution of \(R_n\) at our sample size, without having to derive its limit.

The Idea in Words

  • If we knew \(P\), we would simulate many polls from \(P\) and compute \(R_n\) in each one
  • The \(95\%\) quantile of the simulated \(R_n\)’s would be our \(c\). But we don’t know \(P\)…
  • We do know \(\hat P_n\): the empirical distribution of our 2,000 respondents
  • Bootstrap: simulate polls from \(\hat P_n\) instead of \(P\)
  • Simulating from \(\hat P_n\) = drawing 2,000 respondents with replacement from our sample
  • Efron (1979): you pull yourself up by your bootstraps
  • Dark magic? Do they teach this at Hogwarts? No: it’s just the plug-in principle!
  • Today: the non-parametric bootstrap with iid data

Roots and Pivots

Setup

  • Data: \(X^n = (X_1, \dots, X_n)\), iid with unknown distribution \(P \in \mathcal{P}\)
  • The model \(\mathcal{P}\) can be parametric, semiparametric or nonparametric
  • Parameter of interest: \(\theta(P) \in \Theta \equiv \{\theta(P) : P \in \mathcal{P}\}\)
  • Goal: a random set \(C_n = C_n(X^n)\) such that \(P\{\theta(P) \in C_n\} \approx 1-\alpha\)

Definition (Root)

A root is any real-valued function \(R_n(X^n, \theta(P))\) of the data and of the parameter.

  • The name is due to Beran (1984)
  • Examples: \(\sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n - \theta(P))\) and \(\sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n - \theta(P))/\hat\sigma_n\)
  • In the poll: \(\theta(P)\) stacks the 18 shares, and \(R_n = \max_{s,k}|t_{s,k}|\)

The Object of Interest

Definition (Sampling Distribution of the Root)

\[ J_n(x, P) \equiv P\left\{R_n(X^n, \theta(P)) \le x\right\}, \qquad J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha, P) \equiv \inf\left\{x : J_n(x, P) \ge 1-\alpha\right\} \]

  • \(J_n\) depends on the sample size \(n\) and on the unknown \(P\)
  • The infimum handles discrete distributions, where quantiles may not be unique
  • If we knew \(J_n(\cdot, P)\), we could invert the root: \[ C_n = \left\{\theta \in \Theta : J_n^{-1}(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}, P) \le R_n(X^n, \theta) \le J_n^{-1}(1 - \tfrac{\alpha}{2}, P)\right\} \]
  • By construction, \(P\{\theta(P) \in C_n\} \ge 1 - \alpha\), with equality if \(J_n\) is continuous

From \(J_n\) to an Interval

  • With \(R_n = \sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n - \theta)\), the two inequalities that define \(C_n\) read \[ J_n^{-1}(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}, P) \le \sqrt n\,(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \le J_n^{-1}(1 - \tfrac{\alpha}{2}, P) \]
  • Solve for \(\theta\): \[ \hat\theta_n - \frac{J_n^{-1}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}, P)}{\sqrt n} \le \theta \le \hat\theta_n - \frac{J_n^{-1}(\frac{\alpha}{2}, P)}{\sqrt n} \]
  • Careful with the flip: the upper quantile gives the lower endpoint
  • If \(J_n\) is symmetric around zero, this is just \(\hat\theta_n \pm J_n^{-1}(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}, P)/\sqrt n\)

The goal of this lecture

We want a confidence set for \(\theta(P)\). For that, all we need is \(J_n(\cdot, P)\).

Pivots

Definition (Pivot)

The root \(R_n(X^n, \theta(P))\) is a pivot if \(J_n(x, P)\) does not depend on \(P\): \(J_n(x, P) = J_n(x)\) for all \(P \in \mathcal{P}\).

Examples:

  • \(X_i \iid N(\theta, 1)\): \(\ \sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \theta) \sim N(0,1)\), whatever \(\theta\) is
  • \(X_i \iid N(\mu, \sigma^2)\): \(\ \sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \mu)/S_n \sim t_{n-1}\), whatever \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are
  • Location-scale families, \(X_i \sim F[(x - \theta)/\sigma]\) with \(F\) known: \(\ (\hat\theta_n - \theta)/\hat\sigma_n\)
  • Continuous \(F\): \(\ \sqrt n \sup_x |\hat F_n(x) - F(x)|\) (Kolmogorov–Smirnov)
  • Location-scale needs equivariant \(\hat\theta_n\) and \(\hat\sigma_n\), like \(\bar X_n\) and \(S_n\) (L&R Example 18.2.1)

Why Pivots Are Great

  • Coverage is exact: \(P\{\theta(P) \in C_n\} = 1-\alpha\) for every \(n\) and every \(P \in \mathcal{P}\)
  • No asymptotics at all: \(n = 15\) is as good as \(n = 15{,}000\)
  • Only one distribution to learn: look it up once in a table (think \(t_{n-1}\))
  • No closed form? Simulate it once, from any \(P \in \mathcal{P}\) you like
  • Bootstrap preview: we will replace \(P\) by an estimate \(\hat P_n\)
  • The less \(J_n(\cdot, P)\) depends on \(P\), the less that replacement hurts
  • Moral, and we will come back to it: bootstrap a pivot whenever you can
  • Exercise: show that the location-scale root is a pivot (L&R Problem 18.1)

Asymptotic Pivots

Definition (Asymptotic Pivot)

The root is an asymptotic pivot if \(J_n(\cdot, P)\) converges weakly to a limit \(J\) that does not depend on \(P\).

  • The t-statistic: if \(0 < \sigma^2(P) < \infty\), then \(\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \theta(P))/S_n \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1)\)
  • That’s the TV margin of error: it holds for every \(P\) with finite variance
  • Parametric models: \(n(\hat\theta_n - \theta)'I(\hat\theta_n)(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_k\) (Wald)
  • Recipe: build \(C_n\) with the quantiles of \(J\) instead of those of \(J_n\)
  • The price: coverage is only right in the limit, \(\lim_{n} P\{\theta(P) \in C_n\} = 1-\alpha\)

When the Limit Depends on \(P\)

  • The typical case: \(J_n(\cdot, P) \to J(\cdot, P)\), and the limit depends on \(P\)
  • Example: \(\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \theta(P)) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \sigma^2(P))\), so use \(J(x, \hat P_n) = \Phi(x/\hat\sigma_n)\)
  • This only works if you know \(J(\cdot, P)\) and can estimate whatever it depends on
  • The \(\alpha\)-trimmed mean, for a symmetric \(F\): good luck estimating its asymptotic variance \[ \sigma^2(\alpha, F) = \frac{1}{(1-2\alpha)^2}\left[\int_{F^{-1}(\alpha)}^{F^{-1}(1-\alpha)} \left(t - \theta(F)\right)^2 dF(t) + 2\alpha\left(F^{-1}(\alpha) - \theta(F)\right)^2\right] \]
  • In the poll, \(J(\cdot, P)\) is the law of \(\max_{s,k}|Z_{s,k}|\): no closed form at all
  • Even then, it’s a double approximation: \(J_n(\cdot, P) \approx J(\cdot, P) \approx J(\cdot, \hat P_n)\)

Two Ways to Approximate \(J_n(x, P)\)

\[ \begin{aligned} \textbf{Asymptotics:}&\quad J_n(x, P) \;\underset{n \to \infty}{\approx}\; J(x, P) \;\approx\; J(x, \hat P_n)\\[10pt] \textbf{Bootstrap:}&\quad J_n(x, P) \;\underset{n \to \infty}{\approx}\; J_n(x, \hat P_n) \;\underset{B \to \infty}{\approx}\; L_n(x) \end{aligned} \]

  • Asymptotics: you must know the limit \(J(\cdot, P)\), and then estimate it
  • Bootstrap: estimate \(J_n(\cdot, P)\) directly, by plugging \(\hat P_n\) into \(J_n\) itself
  • \(L_n\) is a simulation-based approximation (more soon): \(B\) is up to you, \(n\) is not

Read this twice

The bootstrap does not avoid asymptotics: it is justified as \(n \to \infty\). What it avoids is having to know \(J(\cdot, P)\).

The Bootstrap

The Bootstrap Estimator

  • Estimate \(P\) by the empirical distribution, which puts mass \(1/n\) on each \(X_i\): \[ \hat P_n(E) \equiv \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n I\{X_i \in E\} \]
  • The bootstrap estimator of the sampling distribution is a plug-in: \(J_n(x, \hat P_n)\)
  • Unusual target: \(J_n(\cdot, P)\) changes with \(n\), and we estimate it at our \(n\)
  • The bootstrap confidence set replaces \(P\) by \(\hat P_n\) in the quantiles: \[ C_n^{*} = \left\{\theta \in \Theta : J_n^{-1}(\tfrac{\alpha}{2}, \hat P_n) \le R_n(X^n, \theta) \le J_n^{-1}(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2}, \hat P_n)\right\} \]
  • Two questions: is \(J_n(\cdot, \hat P_n)\) close to \(J_n(\cdot, P)\)? And how do we compute it?

Why Should \(\hat P_n\) Be Close to \(P\)?

  • With real-valued data, \(\hat F_n(t) \equiv \hat P_n\big((-\infty, t]\big)\) is the empirical CDF
  • Fixed \(t\): \(\hat F_n(t) \to F(t)\) a.s. by the SLLN, since \(\E\left[I\{X_i \le t\}\right] = F(t)\)
  • We can do much better: the convergence is uniform in \(t\)

Theorem (Glivenko–Cantelli)

If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are iid real-valued random variables with CDF \(F\), then \[ \sup_{t \in \R} \left|\hat F_n(t) - F(t)\right| \to 0 \quad \text{almost surely} \]

  • And fast: \(P\left\{\sup_t |\hat F_n(t) - F(t)| > d\right\} \le 2e^{-2nd^2}\) for every \(n\) (DKW inequality)
  • In the poll, \(\hat P_n\) is just 24 cell frequencies: the SLLN alone does the job

Glivenko–Cantelli Works, Baby!

\(X_i \iid U[0,1]\), two independent samples for each \(n\): \(\hat F_n\) is a random function

Is That Enough?

  • Glivenko–Cantelli: \(\hat P_n\) is close to \(P\)
  • But we need more: \(J_n(\cdot, \hat P_n)\) close to \(J_n(\cdot, P)\)
  • That requires \(J_n(\cdot, P)\) to be continuous in \(P\): close inputs, close outputs

Two ingredients, which we will make precise soon:

  1. \(\hat P_n \to P\) in some sense (Glivenko–Cantelli, the SLLN)
  2. Whenever \(P_n \to P\) in that sense, \(J_n(\cdot, P_n) \to J(\cdot, P)\)
  • Ingredient 2 is the delicate one. We will meet a root for which it fails: the maximum!

In Practice

Computing \(J_n(x, \hat P_n)\)

  • Exact computation averages over all \(n^n\) equally likely resamples: hopeless
  • So we simulate. Conditional on the data, for each \(b = 1, \dots, B\):
  1. Draw \(n\) observations with replacement from the data: \(X^{n*}_b = (X^*_{1,b}, \dots, X^*_{n,b})\)
  2. Compute the root at the plug-in parameter: \(R^*_{n,b} = R_n(X^{n*}_b, \hat\theta_n)\), with \(\hat\theta_n = \theta(\hat P_n)\)
  3. Compute the empirical CDF of the \(B\) roots: \(L_n(x) \equiv \frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^B I\{R^*_{n,b} \le x\}\)
  4. Use the quantiles of \(L_n\) in place of those of \(J_n(\cdot, P)\)
  • Two sources of randomness are floating around here. Which ones?

Two Details That Matter

  • Why center at \(\hat\theta_n\)? Under \(\hat P_n\), the “true” parameter is \(\theta(\hat P_n) = \hat\theta_n\)
  • In the poll: bootstrap shares are centered at \(\hat p_{s,k}\), not at the unknown \(p_{s,k}\)
  • How large should \(B\) be? For a fixed sample, \(L_n \to J_n(\cdot, \hat P_n)\) as \(B \to \infty\)
  • \(B\) only kills the simulation error, which is under your control
  • \(n\) drives the statistical error, \(J_n(\cdot, \hat P_n) - J_n(\cdot, P)\): nothing to do about it
  • Tail quantiles need many draws: \(B \ge 1{,}000\) is a reasonable start

In Code

S[i] is the state (0 to 5) and V[i] the vote (0 = A, 1 = B, 2 = C, 3 = other) of respondent i

def shares(S, V):                     # 6 x 3 array with p_hat[s, k]
    return np.array([[np.mean(V[S == s] == k) for k in range(3)] for s in range(6)])

def max_t(S, V, p_ref):               # the root R_n, centered at p_ref
    p = shares(S, V)
    se = np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / np.bincount(S, minlength=6)[:, None])
    return np.max(np.abs(p - p_ref) / se)

def bootstrap_c(S, V, B=1000, alpha=0.05):
    n, p_hat = len(S), shares(S, V)   # under P_hat, the truth is p_hat
    R_star = [max_t(S[i], V[i], p_hat)
              for i in np.random.randint(0, n, size=(B, n))]  # resample rows
    return np.quantile(R_star, 1 - alpha)
  • The joint interval is then p_hat ± bootstrap_c(S, V) * se_hat. That’s it!

Building the Interval

  • Bootstrap quantiles: \(\hat c_q \equiv L_n^{-1}(q)\), from the \(B\) draws of \(R^*_{n,b} = \sqrt n\,(\hat\theta^*_{n,b} - \hat\theta_n)\)
  • Equi-tailed interval, flip included: \[ \left[\hat\theta_n - \hat c_{1-\alpha/2}/\sqrt n,\ \ \hat\theta_n - \hat c_{\alpha/2}/\sqrt n\right] \]
  • Symmetric interval: bootstrap \(|R_n|\) instead, then use \(\hat\theta_n \pm L_n^{-1}(1-\alpha)/\sqrt n\)
  • Joint rectangle (the poll): \(\hat p_{s,k} \pm \hat c^{\,*}\,\widehat{se}_{s,k}\), with \(\hat c^{\,*}\) the \(1-\alpha\) quantile of \(\max_{s,k}|t^*_{s,k}|\)
  • Efron’s percentile interval uses the quantiles of \(\hat\theta^*_{n,b}\) directly: no flip!
  • It’s popular in software, but it is not the interval above
  • Exercise: when does the percentile interval work? (L&R Problem 18.11)

What Is an Observation?

  • In a regression, the unit is the pair \(Z_i = (Y_i, X_i)\): resample rows
  • Pairs bootstrap: draw \(Z^*_1, \dots, Z^*_n\) with replacement, run OLS, store \(\hat\beta^*_b\)
  • Root: \(\sqrt n\,(\hat\beta^*_{b,j} - \hat\beta_j)\) or the t-statistic, exactly as before
  • Bootstrap standard error: the standard deviation of \(\hat\beta^*_{1,j}, \dots, \hat\beta^*_{B,j}\)
  • Never resample \(Y\) and \(X\) separately: that destroys the relation you want to learn!
  • The pairs bootstrap is robust to heteroskedasticity: it mimics the sandwich formula
  • Why it works: \(\hat\beta = \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_iX_i'\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_iY_i\) is a smooth function of means (soon)

function rmultinom(n, pr, binom) {
  const out = new Array(pr.length).fill(0)
  let left = n, mass = 1
  for (let j = 0; j < pr.length - 1 && left > 0; j++) {
    const q = Math.min(1, Math.max(0, pr[j] / mass))
    out[j] = q > 0 ? binom(left, q)() : 0
    left -= out[j]
    mass -= pr[j]
  }
  out[pr.length - 1] += left
  return out
}

// max_{s,k} |t_{s,k}| for 6 states x 4 votes (the first 3 votes are candidates)
function maxT(counts, pRef) {
  let m = 0
  for (let s = 0; s < 6; s++) {
    const N = counts[4 * s] + counts[4 * s + 1] + counts[4 * s + 2] + counts[4 * s + 3]
    for (let k = 0; k < 3; k++) {
      const p = counts[4 * s + k] / N
      const t = Math.abs(p - pRef[3 * s + k]) / Math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / N)
      m = Math.max(m, Number.isNaN(t) ? Infinity : t)
    }
  }
  return m
}

Why It Works

Consistency: The General Recipe

Theorem (L&R 18.3.1)

Let \(\mathcal{C}_P\) be a set of sequences \(\{P_n\}\) that contains \(\{P, P, \dots\}\). Assume that, for every \(\{P_n\} \in \mathcal{C}_P\), \(J_n(\cdot, P_n)\) converges weakly to the same continuous limit \(J(\cdot, P)\). If \(\{\hat P_n\} \in \mathcal{C}_P\) with probability one, then:

  1. \(\ \sup_x \left|J_n(x, \hat P_n) - J_n(x, P)\right| \to 0\) almost surely

  2. \(\ J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha, \hat P_n) \to J^{-1}(1-\alpha, P)\) almost surely, if \(J(\cdot, P)\) is strictly increasing at \(J^{-1}(1-\alpha, P)\)

  3. \(\ P\left\{R_n(X^n, \theta(P)) \le J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha, \hat P_n)\right\} \to 1-\alpha\)

  • Think of \(\mathcal{C}_P\) as the sequences with \(d(P_n, P) \to 0\), for some metric \(d\)
  • Part (iii): the bootstrap confidence set has asymptotically correct coverage

Proof of (i) and (ii)

  • Fix a nonrandom \(\{P_n\} \in \mathcal{C}_P\). Since \(J(\cdot, P)\) is continuous, Pólya’s theorem gives \[ \sup_x |J_n(x, P_n) - J(x, P)| \to 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \sup_x |J_n(x, P) - J(x, P)| \to 0 \]
  • The second one uses the constant sequence \(\{P, P, \dots\} \in \mathcal{C}_P\). By the triangle inequality: \[ \sup_x |J_n(x, P_n) - J_n(x, P)| \le \sup_x |J_n(x, P_n) - J(x, P)| + \sup_x |J(x, P) - J_n(x, P)| \to 0 \]
  • Now the randomness: fix an \(\omega\) in the probability-one event where \(\{\hat P_n(\omega)\} \in \mathcal{C}_P\)
  • Along that path, \(\{\hat P_n(\omega)\}\) is just a nonrandom sequence in \(\mathcal{C}_P\): apply the above \(\implies\) (i)
  • For (ii): if \(F_n \Rightarrow F\), then \(F_n^{-1}(1-\alpha) \to F^{-1}(1-\alpha)\) (L&R Lemma 11.2.1)
  • This needs \(F\) continuous and strictly increasing at \(F^{-1}(1-\alpha)\)
  • Apply it path by path, with \(F_n = J_n(\cdot, \hat P_n(\omega))\) and \(F = J(\cdot, P)\) \(\implies\) (ii)

Proof of (iii)

  • Under \(P\), \(R_n(X^n, \theta(P)) \xrightarrow{d} J(\cdot, P)\): take \(\{P, P, \dots\}\) in the assumption
  • By (ii), \(\hat c_n \equiv J_n^{-1}(1-\alpha, \hat P_n) \to c \equiv J^{-1}(1-\alpha, P)\) almost surely
  • By Slutsky, \(R_n(X^n, \theta(P)) - \hat c_n \xrightarrow{d} J(\cdot + c, P)\), which is continuous at zero, so \[ P\left\{R_n(X^n, \theta(P)) \le \hat c_n\right\} = P\left\{R_n(X^n, \theta(P)) - \hat c_n \le 0\right\} \to J(c, P) = 1 - \alpha \]
  • Bonus: (iii) still holds if (i) and (ii) hold only in probability (L&R Problem 18.3)
  • Almost sure: strongly consistent bootstrap. In probability: weakly consistent

Reading the Theorem

  • All the work is in the assumption: \(J_n(\cdot, P_n) \to J(\cdot, P)\) along every sequence in \(\mathcal{C}_P\)
  • That is a form of continuity of \(J_n\) in \(P\), uniformly in \(n\): “local uniformity”
  • Why sequences? \(\hat P_n\) is random, but each realized path is a nonrandom sequence
  • So: first study nonrandom \(\{P_n\}\), then plug in \(\{\hat P_n\}\) path by path
  • The right notion of “\(P_n \to P\)”, i.e., the set \(\mathcal{C}_P\), depends on the root
  • The bigger \(\mathcal{C}_P\) can be, the easier it is to show that \(\{\hat P_n\} \in \mathcal{C}_P\)
  • Recipe for a new root: (1) find \(\mathcal{C}_P\) with \(J_n(\cdot, P_n) \to J(\cdot, P)\); (2) show \(\{\hat P_n\} \in \mathcal{C}_P\)

The Nonparametric Mean

Theorem (L&R 18.3.3)

Let \(F\) have finite, nonzero variance \(\sigma^2(F)\) and mean \(\theta(F)\). Let \(J_n(\cdot, F)\) be the distribution of \(R_n = \sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \theta(F))\) under \(F\).

  1. Let \(\mathcal{C}_F\) be the set of sequences \(\{F_n\}\) such that \(F_n \Rightarrow F\), \(\theta(F_n) \to \theta(F)\) and \(\sigma^2(F_n) \to \sigma^2(F)\). For every \(\{F_n\} \in \mathcal{C}_F\), \(J_n(\cdot, F_n)\) converges weakly to \(N(0, \sigma^2(F))\).

  2. If \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) are iid \(F\), then the bootstrap \(J_n(\cdot, \hat F_n)\) is strongly consistent: (i)–(iii) of the previous theorem hold.

  • Part (i) is a CLT for a triangular array: row \(n\) is iid from \(F_n\), which changes with \(n\)
  • That’s exactly what resampling from \(\hat F_n\) looks like: a new distribution for each \(n\)

Proof of (i): Setup

  • For each \(n\), let \(X_{n,1}, \dots, X_{n,n}\) be iid \(F_n\), and let \(Y_{n,i} \equiv X_{n,i} - \theta(F_n)\)
  • Since \(\sigma^2(F_n) \to \sigma^2(F) > 0\), Lindeberg–Feller gives \(\sqrt n\, \bar Y_n \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \sigma^2(F))\) as long as \[ \E\left[Y_{n,1}^2\, I\{Y_{n,1}^2 > \epsilon^2 n\}\right] \to 0 \qquad \text{for every } \epsilon > 0 \]
  • The rows are iid, so the Lindeberg condition only involves \(Y_{n,1}\)

What we know about \(Y_{n,1}\):

  • \(Y_{n,1} \xrightarrow{d} Y \equiv X - \theta(F)\), with \(X \sim F\): because \(F_n \Rightarrow F\) and \(\theta(F_n) \to \theta(F)\)
  • \(\E[Y_{n,1}^2] = \sigma^2(F_n) \to \sigma^2(F) = \E[Y^2]\)
  • By the continuous mapping theorem, \(Y_{n,1}^2 \xrightarrow{d} Y^2\)

Proof of (i): Lindeberg

  • Fix \(\beta > 0\), a continuity point of the distribution of \(Y^2\). For all \(n > \beta/\epsilon^2\): \[ \E\left[Y_{n,1}^2\, I\{Y_{n,1}^2 > \epsilon^2 n\}\right] \le \E\left[Y_{n,1}^2\, I\{Y_{n,1}^2 > \beta\}\right] = \E\left[Y_{n,1}^2\right] - \E\left[Y_{n,1}^2\, I\{Y_{n,1}^2 \le \beta\}\right] \]
  • \(Y_{n,1}^2 I\{Y_{n,1}^2 \le \beta\} \xrightarrow{d} Y^2 I\{Y^2 \le \beta\}\): continuous mapping, since \(P\{Y^2 = \beta\} = 0\)
  • These variables live in \([0, \beta]\), so their expectations converge too. Hence \[ \E\left[Y_{n,1}^2\, I\{Y_{n,1}^2 > \beta\}\right] \to \E[Y^2] - \E\left[Y^2 I\{Y^2 \le \beta\}\right] = \E\left[Y^2 I\{Y^2 > \beta\}\right] \]
  • So \(\limsup_n \E\left[Y_{n,1}^2 I\{Y_{n,1}^2 > \epsilon^2 n\}\right] \le \E\left[Y^2 I\{Y^2 > \beta\}\right]\) for every such \(\beta\)
  • Continuity points are dense, so we can let \(\beta \to \infty\) along them
  • Since \(\E[Y^2] < \infty\), dominated convergence gives \(\E[Y^2 I\{Y^2 > \beta\}] \downarrow 0\) \(\blacksquare\)

Proof of (ii)

  • By the previous theorem, it’s enough to show that \(\{\hat F_n\} \in \mathcal{C}_F\) with probability one:
  1. \(\hat F_n \Rightarrow F\) a.s.: Glivenko–Cantelli gives \(\sup_t |\hat F_n(t) - F(t)| \to 0\) a.s.
  2. \(\theta(\hat F_n) = \bar X_n \to \theta(F)\) a.s.: the SLLN, since \(\E|X| < \infty\) when \(\sigma^2(F) < \infty\)
  3. \(\sigma^2(\hat F_n) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i^2 - \bar X_n^2 \to \sigma^2(F)\) a.s.: the SLLN for \(X_i\) and for \(X_i^2\)
  • The intersection of three probability-one events has probability one \(\blacksquare\)
  • In one line: Mallows’ metric \(d_2(\hat F_n, F) \to 0\) a.s. (Bickel and Freedman, 1981)
  • Exercise: show that \(\theta(F_n) \to \theta(F)\) follows from the other two conditions (L&R 18.5)

Beyond the Mean

  • Studentized mean (L&R 18.3.4): \(\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \theta)/\hat\sigma_n\) works too: the bootstrap-t
  • Multivariate mean (L&R 18.3.5): \(\|\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n - \theta)\|\) works for any norm \(\|\cdot\|\)
  • Smooth functions (L&R 18.3.6): \(\sqrt n\,[f(\bar X_n) - f(\theta)]\), \(f\) differentiable: delta method
  • The poll: \(p_{s,k} = P\{S = s, V = k\}/P\{S = s\}\) is a ratio of means, a smooth function
  • The max is continuous, and studentizing works as for the t-stat: our CI is valid
  • OLS: \(\hat\beta = \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_iX_i'\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_iY_i\), also a smooth function of means
  • Reading Hansen? He writes these results as \(\sqrt n\,(\bar Y^* - \bar Y) \xrightarrow{d^*} N(0, \Sigma)\), same idea

When It Fails

The Maximum of a Uniform

  • \(X_i \iid U(0, \theta)\), estimator \(\hat\theta_n = X_{(n)} = \max_i X_i\), root \(R_n = n\,(X_{(n)} - \theta) \le 0\)
  • For \(x \le 0\), the true distribution is \[ J_n(x, P) = P\left\{X_{(n)} \le \theta + \tfrac{x}{n}\right\} = \left(1 + \frac{x}{n\theta}\right)^n \to e^{x/\theta} \]
  • So \(R_n \xrightarrow{d} -\theta \cdot \text{Exp}(1)\): a continuous limit, with no mass at any point
  • Bootstrap root: \(R^*_n = n\,(X^*_{(n)} - X_{(n)}) \le 0\), which is zero if \(X_{(n)}\) is ever drawn
  • Each draw misses \(X_{(n)}\) with probability \(1 - 1/n\), so, given the data, \[ P\left\{R^*_n = 0 \,\middle|\, X^n\right\} = 1 - \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{n}\right)^n \to 1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632 \]
  • The bootstrap puts 63% of its mass on a single point, where the truth puts none!

In Pictures

  • More data does not help: the jump at zero is still there with \(n = 10{,}000\)

What Went Wrong?

  • Ingredient 2 failed: \(J_n(\cdot, P_n) \to J(\cdot, P)\) breaks for sequences \(P_n\) that look like \(\hat P_n\)
  • E.g., \(P_n\) uniform on \(n\) points in \([0, \theta]\), just like \(\hat P_n\): you will prove this in the problem set
  • The known counterexamples share the culprit: a lack of uniformity in \(P\)

Other failures, all with iid data:

  • Infinite variance: for the mean, it works a.s. iff \(\sigma^2 < \infty\) (Giné and Zinn, 1989)
  • Parameter on the boundary: \(\hat\mu_n = \max(\bar X_n, 0)\) when \(\mu = 0\) (L&R Problem 18.10)
  • Degenerate smooth functions: \(\sqrt n\,(\bar X_n^2 - \mu^2)\) when \(\mu = 0\) (L&R Problem 18.17)
  • Superefficient estimators (Hodges): not even the parametric bootstrap works (Beran, 1984)

Handle With Care

If it fails

  • \(m\)-out-of-\(n\): resample only \(m \ll n\)
  • Subsampling (L&R Section 18.7)
  • Find a theorem for your root
  • Bootstrap a pivot when you can

Other bootstraps

  • Parametric: draw from \(P_{\hat\psi}\)
  • Residual: resample OLS residuals
  • Wild: robust to heteroskedasticity
  • Block: dependent data (soon!)
  • Cluster: resample whole clusters

The bottom line

The bootstrap is not magic. Like any asymptotic approximation, it needs a theorem.
