Example: Consumption-Based Asset Pricing

  • A representative agent maximizes expected lifetime utility:

\[ \max_{\{C_t\}_{t}^{\infty}} \E_0\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^t u(C_t) \qquad \text{s.t.} \qquad A_{t+1} = R_{t+1}(A_t - C_t) \]

  • Endogenous variables: consumption \(C_t\) and assets \(A_t\)
  • Exogenous variable: the gross return \(R_{t+1}\), which we observe
  • Parameters: discount factor \(\beta\) and risk aversion \(\gamma\)

Example: From the Euler Equation to a Moment

  • First-order condition (Euler equation):

\[ u'(C_t) = \beta\,\E_t\left[R_{t+1}\,u'(C_{t+1})\right] \]

  • With CRRA utility, \(u'(C) = C^{-\gamma}\). Divide both sides by \(C_t^{-\gamma}\):

\[ \E_t\Bigg[\underbrace{\beta R_{t+1}\left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\gamma} - 1}_{\equiv\, h(\text{parameters, data})}\Bigg] = 0 \implies \E\left[h(\text{parameters, data})\right] = 0 \]

  • No distribution assumed for consumption or returns. No need to solve the model!

The General Framework

  • Let \(\symbf{w}_t\) be the \(p\times 1\) vector of variables observed at time \(t\)
  • Let \(\symbf{\theta}\) be the \(a\times 1\) vector of parameters to be estimated
  • Let \(\symbf{h}: \R^a\times\R^p\to\R^r\) be a known vector-valued function
  • We assume there is a true value \(\symbf{\theta}_0\) such that:

\[ \E\left[\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}_0, \symbf{w}_t)\right] = \symbf{0}_{r\times 1} \]

  • \(\symbf{m}(\symbf{\theta}) \equiv \E\left[\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}, \symbf{w}_t)\right]\) is just a function of \(\symbf{\theta}\)
  • Idea: let’s find a root of \(\symbf{m}(\symbf{\theta})\)! What are the problems with this idea?

Two Problems

  • We do not know how to compute that expectation
  • \(\symbf{m}(\symbf{\theta}) = \symbf{0}\) may have no solution, or many: think about \(a > r\), \(a = r\) and \(a < r\)
  • Hansen (1982) proposed a very clever way to deal with both
  • First: approximate the expectation by its sample analogue:

\[ \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}) \equiv \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}, \symbf{w}_t) \]

  • This is where dependence will enter: an average of a dependent sequence

The GMM Estimator

  • Second: if we cannot make \(\hat{\symbf{m}}_T\) equal to zero, let’s minimize its distance to zero!
  • Let \(\symbf{W}_T\) be an \(r\times r\) positive definite matrix, and define:

\[ Q_T(\symbf{\theta}) \equiv \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta})'\,\symbf{W}_T\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}) \ge 0 \]

  • The GMM estimator is the minimizer of this criterion:

\[ \hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T \equiv \arg\min_{\symbf{\theta}\in\Theta} Q_T(\symbf{\theta}) \]

  • The argmin depends on the weighting matrix \(\symbf{W}_T\), by the way
  • Intuition: near \(\symbf{\theta}_0\), \(Q_T \approx 0\) by the LLN, if \(\symbf{h}\) is smooth enough

The First-Order Conditions

  • Let \(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T(\symbf{\theta}) \equiv \frac{\partial\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta})}{\partial\symbf{\theta}'} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}, \symbf{w}_t)}{\partial\symbf{\theta}'}\), an \(r\times a\) matrix
  • For a differentiable \(\symbf{h}\), the FOC for the GMM estimator is:

\[ \frac{\partial Q_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T)}{\partial\symbf{\theta}} = 2\,\hat{\symbf{D}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T)'\,\symbf{W}_T\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T) = \symbf{0}_{a\times 1} \]

  • Even for a fixed \(\symbf{W}_T\), mild conditions guarantee a consistent estimator
  • The proofs are fairly similar to those for the ML estimator
  • See Newey and McFadden (1994) for all the details you would ever want to know
  • From here on, assume \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{\theta}_0\), and let’s study the asymptotic distribution

Asymptotic Theory

Expanding the Sample Moments

  • Goal: the asymptotic distribution of \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T\)
  • We haven’t said anything about the sampling of \(\symbf{w}_t\) yet!
  • Assume each entry \(\hat m_{i,T}\) of \(\hat{\symbf{m}}_T\) is continuously differentiable in \(\symbf{\theta}\)
  • Apply the mean value theorem to each entry, with \(\tilde{\symbf{\theta}}_{i,T}\) between \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T\) and \(\symbf{\theta}_0\):

\[ \hat m_{i,T}(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T) = \hat m_{i,T}(\symbf{\theta}_0) + \frac{\partial\hat m_{i,T}(\tilde{\symbf{\theta}}_{i,T})}{\partial\symbf{\theta}'}\left(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T - \symbf{\theta}_0\right) \]

Stacking the Expansions

  • Stack the \(r\) expansions:

\[ \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T) = \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0) + \tilde{\symbf{D}}_T\left(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T - \symbf{\theta}_0\right), \qquad \tilde{\symbf{D}}_T \equiv \begin{bmatrix}\partial\hat m_{1,T}(\tilde{\symbf{\theta}}_{1,T})/\partial\symbf{\theta}' \\ \vdots \\ \partial\hat m_{r,T}(\tilde{\symbf{\theta}}_{r,T})/\partial\symbf{\theta}'\end{bmatrix} \]

  • In general, \(\tilde{\symbf{D}}_T\) is not the Jacobian \(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T)\). Why?
  • But every \(\tilde{\symbf{\theta}}_{i,T}\) is squeezed between \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T\) and \(\symbf{\theta}_0\), so \(\tilde{\symbf{\theta}}_{i,T} \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{\theta}_0\) for all \(i\)
  • Assume uniform convergence, so that \(\tilde{\symbf{D}}_T\) and \(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T)\) both tend to

\[ \symbf{D} \equiv \left.\frac{\partial\symbf{m}(\symbf{\theta})}{\partial\symbf{\theta}'}\right|_{\symbf{\theta}=\symbf{\theta}_0}, \qquad r\times a \text{ with full column rank} \]

Using the FOC

  • Write \(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T \equiv \hat{\symbf{D}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T)\) and multiply the expansion by \(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\):

\[ \hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T) = \hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0) + \hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\tilde{\symbf{D}}_T\left(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T - \symbf{\theta}_0\right) \]

  • But the LHS is zero by the FOC!!!
  • Rearrange and scale by \(\sqrt{T}\):

\[ \sqrt{T}\left(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T - \symbf{\theta}_0\right) = -\left(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\tilde{\symbf{D}}_T\right)^{-1}\hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\cdot\sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0) \]

  • Assume \(\symbf{W}_T \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{W}\), positive definite. Then, by Slutsky:

\[ \hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\tilde{\symbf{D}}_T \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D} \]

Where Dependence Enters

  • We are left with \(\sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\symbf{h}_t\), where \(\symbf{h}_t \equiv \symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}_0, \symbf{w}_t)\)
  • It has mean zero. Why? This is exactly lecture 7 with \(\symbf{g}_t = \symbf{h}_t\)!
  • Assume the conditions of one of our CLTs for dependent data. Then:

\[ \frac{1}{\sqrt{T}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\symbf{h}_t \xrightarrow{d} N(\symbf{0}, \symbf{S}), \qquad \symbf{S} \equiv \sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\E\left[\symbf{h}_t\symbf{h}_{t-j}'\right] \]

  • \(\symbf{S}\) is the long-run variance \(\symbf{\Omega}\) of lecture 7. I keep the letter that GMM people use
  • \(\symbf{h}_t\) i.i.d. or an MDS: only \(j = 0\) survives, so \(\symbf{S} = \E[\symbf{h}_t\symbf{h}_t']\), the formula you know
  • This is the only place in the argument where the sampling of \(\symbf{w}_t\) matters

The Asymptotic Distribution

Theorem (Asymptotic Distribution of GMM)

Under the assumptions above, \[ \sqrt{T}\left(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T - \symbf{\theta}_0\right) \xrightarrow{d} N\left(\symbf{0}, \symbf{V}\right), \qquad \symbf{V} \equiv \left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{S}\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1} \]

  • A sandwich again: bread from \(\symbf{D}\) and \(\symbf{W}\), meat from \(\symbf{S}\)
  • Very important: the asymptotic variance depends on the weighting matrix \(\symbf{W}\)!
  • Consistency holds for any positive definite \(\symbf{W}\)
  • If \(r = a\), \(\symbf{W}\) drops out: \(\symbf{V} = \symbf{D}^{-1}\symbf{S}(\symbf{D}')^{-1}\)

The Optimal Weighting Matrix

A Thought Experiment

  • Suppose you want to know where an enemy plane is, and you have two radars
  • Both are consistent “estimators”, but radar 1 is noisier than radar 2
  • What’s the optimal thing to do? Use only radar 1? Only radar 2? Both?
  • You’d probably use both, but give more weight to radar 2
  • This is exactly the idea behind the optimal weighting matrix in GMM!
  • How far each sample moment is from zero = your radar when looking for \(\symbf{\theta}_0\)
  • How you combine them = the weighting matrix \(\symbf{W}\)

How to Combine the Radars?

  • Intuition: give more weight to “more precise” moment conditions
  • How to measure precision? With the variance-covariance matrix of the moments
  • Uncorrelated moments: just use the inverse of the variances
  • Correlated moments: use the inverse of the variance-covariance matrix
  • This is exactly what Hansen (1982) proposed!
  • Nothing in this intuition has to do with i.i.d. versus dependent data
  • The relevant variance is the one of \(\sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0)\): that is why it is the long-run variance \(\symbf{S}\)

Let’s Do the Math

  • The asymptotic variance of the GMM estimator is:

\[ \symbf{V} = \left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{S}\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1} \]

  • If \(\symbf{W} = \symbf{S}^{-1}\), the sandwich collapses:

\[ \symbf{V}^* = \left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{S}\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1} = \left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1} \]

  • How can we compare how “large” \(\symbf{V}\) and \(\symbf{V}^*\) are?
  • We write \(\symbf{V} \succeq \symbf{V}^*\) if, and only if, \(\symbf{V} - \symbf{V}^*\) is positive semi-definite
  • Claim: \(\symbf{V} \succeq \symbf{V}^*\) for every positive definite \(\symbf{W}\)

Factoring the Difference

  • Pull out the bread on both sides:

\[ \symbf{V} - \symbf{V}^* = \left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\left[\symbf{S} - \symbf{D}\left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\right]\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1} \]

  • Write \(\symbf{S} = \symbf{S}^{1/2}\symbf{S}^{1/2}\). Why is this well-defined?
  • Define \(\symbf{A} \equiv \left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{W}\symbf{S}^{1/2}\) and \(\symbf{B} \equiv \symbf{S}^{-1/2}\symbf{D}\left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1/2}\)
  • Then factor \(\symbf{S}^{1/2}\) out of the bracket on each side:

\[ \symbf{V} - \symbf{V}^* = \symbf{A}\left(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B}\right)\symbf{A}' \]

The Magic of Idempotent Matrices

  • It is enough to show that \(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B}\) is positive semi-definite
  • \(\symbf{B}\) is symmetric and idempotent: \(\symbf{B}^2 = \symbf{B}\). What do we know about such matrices?
  • All eigenvalues of an idempotent matrix are either 0 or 1
  • So all eigenvalues of \(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B}\) are either 0 or 1, and it is positive semi-definite
  • Equivalently: \(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B} = (\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B})(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B})'\)

Theorem (Optimal Weighting Matrix)

\(\symbf{V} - \symbf{V}^* = \symbf{A}(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B})\symbf{A}' \succeq 0\) for every positive definite \(\symbf{W}\), so \(\symbf{W} = \symbf{S}^{-1}\) is optimal: the inverse of the long-run variance of the moments.

Efficient GMM in Practice

The Big Problem

  • We want \(\symbf{W}_T \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{S}^{-1}\), but we do not observe \(\symbf{S}\)
  • Worse: \(\symbf{S}\) depends on \(\symbf{\theta}_0\), the object we want to estimate!
  • Very important: consistency does not depend on using the optimal matrix
  • So start from any initial estimate and form the “residuals” \(\hat{\symbf{h}}_t \equiv \symbf{h}(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T, \symbf{w}_t)\)
  • Then estimate \(\symbf{S}\) from them, exactly as in lecture 7

Estimating \(\symbf{S}\)

  • \(\symbf{h}_t\) i.i.d. or an MDS: use White’s estimator

\[ \hat{\symbf{S}}_T = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat{\symbf{h}}_t\hat{\symbf{h}}_t' \]

  • Serially correlated \(\symbf{h}_t\): use Newey-West, with \(\hat{\symbf{g}}_t = \hat{\symbf{h}}_t\)

\[ \hat{\symbf{S}}_T = \hat{\symbf{\Gamma}}_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\omega_j\left(\hat{\symbf{\Gamma}}_j + \hat{\symbf{\Gamma}}_j'\right), \qquad \omega_j = 1 - \frac{j}{q+1}, \qquad \hat{\symbf{\Gamma}}_j = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=j+1}^{T}\hat{\symbf{h}}_t\hat{\symbf{h}}_{t-j}' \]

  • Same advice as lecture 7: \(q \propto T^{1/3}\), and report it
  • Now positive semi-definiteness really bites: we are about to invert this matrix

The Two-Step GMM Procedure

  • Step 1: pick any positive definite \(\symbf{W}_T^{(1)}\), e.g., the identity matrix \(\symbf{I}_r\)

\[ \hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(1)} = \arg\min_{\symbf{\theta}}\ \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta})'\,\symbf{W}_T^{(1)}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}) \]

  • Step 2: compute \(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T\) from \(\hat{\symbf{h}}_t = \symbf{h}(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(1)}, \symbf{w}_t)\), by White or by Newey-West
  • Step 3: set \(\symbf{W}_T^{(2)} = \hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{-1}\) and minimize again:

\[ \hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(2)} = \arg\min_{\symbf{\theta}}\ \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta})'\,\hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{-1}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}) \]

  • Result: \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(2)}\) is asymptotically efficient!

Why Does Two-Step GMM Work?

  • Key insight: consistency of \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(1)}\) does not require optimal weighting
  • Since \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(1)} \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{\theta}_0\), we have \(\hat{\symbf{h}}_t \approx \symbf{h}_t\), and so \(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{S}\)
  • By continuous mapping, \(\symbf{W}_T^{(2)} = \hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{-1} \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{S}^{-1}\)
  • Our theorem only needs \(\symbf{W}_T \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{W}\): the limit is all that matters
  • So \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(2)}\) has asymptotic variance \(\symbf{V}^*\)
  • Estimating \(\symbf{S}\) costs nothing in the limit. In finite samples, it does

Iterated GMM

  • Why stop at two steps? Keep iterating until convergence:
  • Start from \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(1)}\). For \(k = 2, 3, \ldots\):
    1. Compute \(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{(k)}\) using residuals from \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(k-1)}\)
    2. Update: \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(k)} = \arg\min_{\symbf{\theta}}\ \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta})'\left(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{(k)}\right)^{-1}\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta})\)
    3. Stop when \(\|\hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(k)} - \hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{(k-1)}\| < \epsilon\), where you pick \(\epsilon\)
  • Same asymptotic distribution as two-step GMM
  • Potential finite-sample improvements, but results are mixed in the literature
  • Computationally more expensive

Continuously-Updated GMM (CUE)

  • Alternative: update the weighting matrix at each function evaluation:

\[ \hat{\symbf{\theta}}^{CUE} = \arg\min_{\symbf{\theta}}\ \hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta})'\,\hat{\symbf{S}}_T(\symbf{\theta})^{-1}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}) \]

  • Same asymptotic distribution as two-step and iterated GMM. So why bother?
  • ● No first step: no arbitrary initial weighting matrix to choose
  • ● Invariant to linear transformations of the moments: units do not matter
  • ● Less finite-sample bias (Hansen, Heaton and Yaron (1996))
  • ● Objective at \(\symbf{\theta}_0\), times \(T\): \(\chi^2_r\) even if identification is weak (Stock and Wright (2000))
  • ● Fat tails: it can lack finite moments (Hausman et al. (2011))
  • ● Computationally intensive, and optimizers can miss the global minimum
  • Linear IV: the global minimum comes from polynomial roots (Moreira et al. (2026))

Standard Errors

  • Estimate \(\symbf{D}\) by the sample Jacobian at the estimate: \(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T = \hat{\symbf{D}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T)\)
  • Estimate \(\symbf{S}\) by \(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T\), from residuals at \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T\)
  • Efficient GMM:

\[ \hat{\symbf{V}}^* = \left(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{-1}\hat{\symbf{D}}_T\right)^{-1}, \qquad \text{standard errors: square roots of the diagonal of } \hat{\symbf{V}}^*/T \]

  • Any other \(\symbf{W}_T\) (e.g., your first step): the full sandwich!

\[ \hat{\symbf{V}} = \left(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\hat{\symbf{D}}_T\right)^{-1}\hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\hat{\symbf{S}}_T\symbf{W}_T\hat{\symbf{D}}_T\left(\hat{\symbf{D}}_T'\symbf{W}_T\hat{\symbf{D}}_T\right)^{-1} \]

  • Usual \(t\)-tests and Wald tests are valid

The J-Test

Testing the Model

  • GMM allows \(r > a\), more moments than parameters: overidentification
  • If the model is correct, all \(r\) sample moments should be “close to zero”
  • But we can only set \(a\) linear combinations of them to zero through \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T\)
  • That leaves \(r - a\) overidentifying restrictions
  • Idea: test whether the criterion at the minimum is “too large”
  • Correct model: it should be small
  • Wrong model: the moments are incompatible, and it will be large

The J-Statistic

Theorem (Hansen’s J-Test)

Let \(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T^*\) be the efficient GMM estimator, with \(\symbf{W}_T = \hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{-1}\) and \(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T \xrightarrow{p} \symbf{S}\). If the \(r\) moment conditions hold, \[ J_T \equiv T\cdot\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T^*)'\,\hat{\symbf{S}}_T^{-1}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T^*) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{r-a} \]

  • Degrees of freedom: the number of overidentifying restrictions
  • Reject the model if \(J_T\) exceeds the \(1-\alpha\) quantile of \(\chi^2_{r-a}\): a right-tail test
  • You must use the efficient weighting matrix for the \(\chi^2\) result to hold!
  • If \(r = a\), then \(J_T = 0\): there is nothing to test
  • Proof in the appendix: the same idempotent matrix as in the efficiency proof

What If We Reject?

  • Rejection means: the moment conditions are incompatible with the data
  • Possible interpretations:
    • The model is misspecified (wrong functional form, missing variables)
    • Some moment conditions are invalid
    • Finite-sample issues: the test can over-reject in small samples
  • Important caveats:
    • It is an omnibus test: many different violations can trigger a rejection
    • It is not diagnostic: it does not tell you which moment condition is wrong
    • You need economic theory or additional tests to diagnose the problem
    • Failing to reject does not prove the model is correct!

Conditional Moments and Instruments

Revisiting the Consumption Model

  • The Euler equation is a conditional moment restriction:

\[ \E\left[\beta R_{t+1}\left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\gamma} - 1 \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_t\right] = 0 \]

  • Define the moment function, with \(\symbf{\theta} = (\beta, \gamma)'\):

\[ h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}) \equiv \beta R_{t+1}\left(\frac{C_{t+1}}{C_t}\right)^{-\gamma} - 1 \]

  • This says: “given what we know at \(t\), the expected value of \(h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\) is zero”
  • One equation, two parameters. Is that a problem?

From Conditional to Unconditional Moments

  • The law of iterated expectations tells us something powerful:

\[ \E\left[h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\mid\mathcal{F}_t\right] = 0 \implies \E\left[z_t\,h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\right] = 0 \]

for any variable \(z_t\) known at time \(t\) (that is, \(\mathcal{F}_t\)-measurable, with the moments existing)

  • Why? Pull out what is known at \(t\):

\[ \E\left[z_t\,h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\right] = \E\Big[\E\left[z_t\,h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\mid\mathcal{F}_t\right]\Big] = \E\Big[z_t\underbrace{\E\left[h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\mid\mathcal{F}_t\right]}_{=\,0}\Big] = 0 \]

  • We can create many unconditional moments from one conditional restriction!
  • The \(z_t\) are called instruments

Examples of Valid Instruments

  • In the consumption model, \(\mathcal{F}_t\) includes past consumption, returns and other observables
  • All of these are valid moment conditions:
    • \(\E\left[1\cdot h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\right] = 0\)
    • \(\E\left[(C_t/C_{t-1})\,h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\right] = 0\)
    • \(\E\left[R_t\,h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\right] = 0\)
    • \(\E\left[R_t^2\,h_{t+1}(\symbf{\theta}_0)\right] = 0\)
  • With \(\ell\) instruments: \(r = \ell\) moments for \(a = 2\) parameters
  • Exactly identified if \(\ell = 2\), overidentified if \(\ell > 2\) (we can use the J-test!)
  • \(C_t\) in levels? Valid by the LIE, but it trends: no LLN or CLT from lecture 7 applies

General Framework

  • Many models specify, with \(\symbf{h}\) a \(k\times 1\) vector (e.g., one Euler equation per asset):

\[ \E\left[\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}_0, \symbf{w}_{t+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_t\right] = \symbf{0} \]

  • Let \(\symbf{z}_t\) be an \(\ell\times 1\) vector of variables known at \(t\). By the LIE:

\[ \E\left[\symbf{z}_t\otimes\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}_0, \symbf{w}_{t+1})\right] = \symbf{0} \]

  • The Kronecker product creates all interactions, so \(r = \ell\cdot k\) moments:

\[ \symbf{z}_t\otimes\symbf{h}_{t+1} = \begin{bmatrix} z_{1,t}\,\symbf{h}_{t+1} \\ \vdots \\ z_{\ell,t}\,\symbf{h}_{t+1}\end{bmatrix} \]

Do We Even Need HAC?

  • Let \(\symbf{g}_{t+1} \equiv \symbf{z}_t\otimes\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}_0, \symbf{w}_{t+1})\), the moments at the true parameter
  • Since \(\symbf{z}_t\) is known at \(t\):

\[ \E\left[\symbf{g}_{t+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_t\right] = \symbf{z}_t\otimes\E\left[\symbf{h}(\symbf{\theta}_0, \symbf{w}_{t+1})\mid\mathcal{F}_t\right] = \symbf{0} \]

  • So \(\{\symbf{g}_{t+1}\}\) is an MDS: every autocovariance is zero, and \(\symbf{S} = \E\left[\symbf{g}_{t+1}\symbf{g}_{t+1}'\right]\)
  • The model itself tells you: White’s estimator is enough!
  • This is the Scores are an MDS under your null advice from lecture 7
  • Newey-West stays consistent here too: cheap insurance, at the cost of some noise

When Do We Need HAC?

  • Overlapping horizons. Take the \(n\)-period version of the Euler equation:

\[ \E\left[\beta^n R_{t,t+n}\left(\frac{C_{t+n}}{C_t}\right)^{-\gamma} - 1 \,\middle|\, \mathcal{F}_t\right] = 0 \]

  • Here \(R_{t,t+n}\) is the \(n\)-period gross return
  • Now \(\symbf{g}_{t+n}\) is known at \(t+n\), and it has mean zero given \(\mathcal{F}_t\)
  • So \(\symbf{\Gamma}_j = \symbf{0}\) for \(j \ge n\), but lags \(1, \ldots, n-1\) survive (Hansen and Hodrick (1980))
  • Moments with no conditional structure: no MDS to lean on
  • In both cases: Newey-West, with the same \(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T\) as before

The Instruments Trade-Off

More instruments (larger \(\ell\)):

  • ● More information, and potentially more efficient estimates
  • ● More overidentifying restrictions to test
  • ● A larger \(\hat{\symbf{S}}_T\) to invert
  • ● Weak or irrelevant instruments can screw up everything!

Fewer instruments (smaller \(\ell\)):

  • ● Simpler estimation, and less finite-sample bias
  • ● Focus on the strongest moment conditions
  • ● Less efficient asymptotically
  • ● Cannot test overidentification if \(r = a\)

Practical Guidance

How to choose which functions of \(\mathcal{F}_t\) to use?

  • Theory first: use economic theory to guide which variables should be relevant
  • Parsimony: start with a small set of the most relevant instruments
  • Balance: enough moments to test, but not so many that bias becomes severe
  • Robustness: check sensitivity to different instrument sets

Example from the consumption model:

  • Conservative: a constant and \(C_t/C_{t-1}\). Exactly identified: nothing to test
  • Moderate: add \(R_t\) and a lag of each. Overidentified, so we can test!
  • Aggressive: many lags and transformations. Beware of finite-sample issues

Chamberlain’s Efficiency Bound

  • Is \(\symbf{V}^*\) the best we can do? A fundamental result by Chamberlain (1987):
  • With i.i.d. data, \(\symbf{V}^*\) is the semiparametric efficiency bound
  • No regular estimator using only these moments has a smaller asymptotic variance
  • Regular: small changes in the true distribution do not change its limit distribution
  • That rules out tricks that beat the bound at a single parameter value
  • To do better, you need stronger assumptions: with a full likelihood, MLE might beat GMM
  • If you only trust your moment conditions, efficient GMM is optimal!

Is GMM Still the Best with Time Series?

  • Chamberlain assumes i.i.d. data. What if today’s data depend on yesterday’s?
  • Good news: \(\symbf{S}^{-1}\) is still the best weighting matrix. Our proof never used independence
  • Back to the radars: this is the best way to combine the radars you already own
  • But you choose the radars! Each instrument in \(\symbf{z}_t\) is one more radar
  • Different \(\symbf{z}_t\), different \(\symbf{V}^*\). So also ask: which radars should we get?
  • Hansen (1985), Hansen, Heaton and Ogaki (1988): how low can \(\symbf{V}^*\) go across all \(\symbf{z}_t\)?
  • Could a totally different estimator beat GMM with the best radars?
  • If only today matters for tomorrow (Markov data), no (Carrasco and Florens (2014))
  • The catch: getting there may take infinitely many moments

Classical Estimators as GMM

A Unifying Framework

  • Many familiar estimators are special cases of GMM
  • We will see OLS, IV/2SLS, GLS, NLS and MLE
  • In each case, ask what the moment function is
  • Most of them are exactly identified. So \(\symbf{W}\) is irrelevant, and:

\[ \symbf{V} = \symbf{D}^{-1}\symbf{S}\left(\symbf{D}'\right)^{-1} \]

  • Asymptotic variance for free, with dependence handled by \(\symbf{S}\)
  • It also suggests robustness checks: e.g., change the weighting matrix

OLS as GMM

  • Linear model: \(y_t = \symbf{x}_t'\symbf{\beta}_0 + u_t\) with \(\E[\symbf{x}_t u_t] = \symbf{0}\)
  • Moment function: \(\symbf{h}(\symbf{\beta}, \symbf{w}_t) = \symbf{x}_t(y_t - \symbf{x}_t'\symbf{\beta})\)
  • \(r = a = \dim(\symbf{x}_t)\): exactly identified, so the weighting matrix doesn’t matter!
  • Set the sample moment to zero:

\[ \sum_{t=1}^{T}\symbf{x}_t\left(y_t - \symbf{x}_t'\hat{\symbf{\beta}}\right) = \symbf{0} \implies \hat{\symbf{\beta}}_{OLS} = \left(\symbf{X}'\symbf{X}\right)^{-1}\symbf{X}'\symbf{y} \]

  • \(\symbf{D} = -\E[\symbf{x}_t\symbf{x}_t'] = -\symbf{Q}\), and \(\symbf{S}\) is the long-run variance of the scores \(\symbf{x}_t u_t\)
  • So \(\symbf{V} = \symbf{Q}^{-1}\symbf{S}\symbf{Q}^{-1}\): the HAC formula from lecture 7

IV and 2SLS as GMM

  • Linear model with instruments: \(y_t = \symbf{x}_t'\symbf{\beta}_0 + u_t\) with \(\E[\symbf{z}_t u_t] = \symbf{0}\) and \(\symbf{z}_t \in \R^\ell\)
  • Moment function: \(\symbf{h}(\symbf{\beta}, \symbf{w}_t) = \symbf{z}_t(y_t - \symbf{x}_t'\symbf{\beta})\)
  • Exactly identified, \(\ell = \dim(\symbf{x}_t)\): any \(\symbf{W}_T\) gives \(\hat{\symbf{\beta}}_{IV} = (\symbf{Z}'\symbf{X})^{-1}\symbf{Z}'\symbf{y}\)
  • Overidentified, \(\ell > \dim(\symbf{x}_t)\): the choice \(\symbf{W}_T = \left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_t\symbf{z}_t\symbf{z}_t'\right)^{-1}\) gives 2SLS, in one step
  • With \(\symbf{P}_{\symbf{Z}} \equiv \symbf{Z}(\symbf{Z}'\symbf{Z})^{-1}\symbf{Z}'\): \(\ \hat{\symbf{\beta}}_{2SLS} = \left(\symbf{X}'\symbf{P}_{\symbf{Z}}\symbf{X}\right)^{-1}\symbf{X}'\symbf{P}_{\symbf{Z}}\symbf{y}\)
  • Scores an MDS and homoskedastic? \(\symbf{S} = \sigma^2\E[\symbf{z}_t\symbf{z}_t']\), so 2SLS is efficient GMM
  • Otherwise, two-step GMM with White or Newey-West beats 2SLS

GLS as GMM

  • Linear model with known \(\E[\symbf{u}\symbf{u}'\mid\symbf{X}] = \symbf{\Omega}\) and \(\E[\symbf{u}\mid\symbf{X}] = \symbf{0}\)
  • Careful: \(\symbf{X}'(\symbf{y} - \symbf{X}\symbf{\beta})\) is exactly identified, so it gives OLS for every weighting matrix!
  • The efficiency gain comes from changing the moments, not the weights
  • \(\E[\symbf{u}\mid\symbf{X}] = \symbf{0}\) makes any function of \(\symbf{X}\) a valid instrument. Take \(\symbf{\Omega}^{-1}\symbf{X}\):

\[ \symbf{X}'\symbf{\Omega}^{-1}\left(\symbf{y} - \symbf{X}\hat{\symbf{\beta}}\right) = \symbf{0} \implies \hat{\symbf{\beta}}_{GLS} = \left(\symbf{X}'\symbf{\Omega}^{-1}\symbf{X}\right)^{-1}\symbf{X}'\symbf{\Omega}^{-1}\symbf{y} \]

  • Estimated \(\hat{\symbf{\Omega}}\) in place of \(\symbf{\Omega}\): FGLS
  • With serial correlation, these moments mix \(\symbf{x}_s\) and \(u_t\) across dates
  • So GLS needs strict exogeneity, which fails with lagged dependent variables

NLS as GMM

  • Nonlinear regression: \(y_t = g(\symbf{x}_t, \symbf{\beta}_0) + u_t\) with \(\E[u_t\mid\symbf{x}_t] = 0\)
  • NLS minimizes \(\sum_t\left(y_t - g(\symbf{x}_t, \symbf{\beta})\right)^2\). Its first-order condition is a moment condition!
  • Moment function, with \(\symbf{g}_{\symbf{\beta}}(\symbf{x}_t, \symbf{\beta}) \equiv \partial g(\symbf{x}_t, \symbf{\beta})/\partial\symbf{\beta}\):

\[ \symbf{h}(\symbf{\beta}, \symbf{w}_t) = \symbf{g}_{\symbf{\beta}}(\symbf{x}_t, \symbf{\beta})\left(y_t - g(\symbf{x}_t, \symbf{\beta})\right) \]

  • Exactly identified: \(r = a = \dim(\symbf{\beta})\). Valid because \(\E[u_t\mid\symbf{x}_t] = 0\), by the LIE
  • \(\E[\symbf{x}_t u_t] = \symbf{0}\) alone does not give NLS: that is a different moment function
  • \(\symbf{D} = -\E\left[\symbf{g}_{\symbf{\beta}}\symbf{g}_{\symbf{\beta}}'\right]\) at \(\symbf{\beta}_0\). The variance comes directly from GMM theory

MLE as GMM

  • Let \(\symbf{s}_t(\symbf{\theta}) \equiv \partial\log f_t(\symbf{\theta})/\partial\symbf{\theta}\) be the score of observation \(t\)
  • The MLE solves \(\sum_t\symbf{s}_t(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}) = \symbf{0}\): exactly identified GMM with \(\symbf{h} = \symbf{s}_t\)!
  • \(\symbf{D}\) is the expected Hessian: the bread of lecture 6’s sandwich
  • \(\symbf{S}\) is the long-run variance of the scores: the meat
  • Scores an MDS? Then \(\symbf{S}\) is the outer product of the score, as in lecture 6
  • Serially correlated scores in a quasi-MLE? Use Newey-West for the meat
  • So MLE is GMM with moments chosen by a fully specified model
  • Correct likelihood: the score is the best moment function you could pick

Takeaways

What to Remember

  • GMM minimizes a quadratic form in sample moments. Any \(\symbf{W} \succ 0\) gives consistency
  • Under dependence, \(\symbf{S}\) is a long-run variance. The optimal weighting matrix is \(\symbf{S}^{-1}\)
  • Two-step GMM is efficient: estimate \(\symbf{S}\) with White or Newey-West, then re-minimize
  • With \(r > a\), the J-test checks the model: \(J_T \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{r-a}\)
  • Conditional moments create instruments, and their one-step moments are an MDS

Proof: The Limit of the J-Statistic

  • Plug \(\sqrt{T}(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T^* - \symbf{\theta}_0)\), with \(\symbf{W} = \symbf{S}^{-1}\), into the expansion of \(\hat{\symbf{m}}_T\):

\[ \sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T^*) = \left[\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{D}\left(\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\right)^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\right]\sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0) + o_p(1) \]

  • Premultiply by \(\symbf{S}^{-1/2}\), and insert \(\symbf{S}^{1/2}\symbf{S}^{-1/2}\) before \(\sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0)\):

\[ \symbf{S}^{-1/2}\sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\hat{\symbf{\theta}}_T^*) = \left(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B}\right)\symbf{Z}_T + o_p(1), \qquad \symbf{Z}_T \equiv \symbf{S}^{-1/2}\sqrt{T}\,\hat{\symbf{m}}_T(\symbf{\theta}_0) \xrightarrow{d} N(\symbf{0}, \symbf{I}_r) \]

  • \(\symbf{B}\) is the idempotent matrix from the efficiency proof. Then, by Slutsky:

\[ J_T = \symbf{Z}_T'\left(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B}\right)\symbf{Z}_T + o_p(1) \xrightarrow{d} \chi^2_{\operatorname{tr}(\symbf{I}_r - \symbf{B})} = \chi^2_{r-a} \]

  • \(\operatorname{tr}(\symbf{B}) = \operatorname{tr}\left[(\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D})^{-1}\symbf{D}'\symbf{S}^{-1}\symbf{D}\right] = \operatorname{tr}(\symbf{I}_a) = a\)
