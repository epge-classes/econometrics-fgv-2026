ARMA(p,q) Models

The ARMA(p,q) Model

  • Let \(\varepsilon_t\) be a white noise with variance \(\sigma^2\)
  • An ARMA\((p,q)\) process \(y_t\) satisfies

\[ y_t = \mu + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q} \]

  • Equivalently, in lag-operator notation:

\[ \phi(L)\, y_t = \mu + \theta(L)\, \varepsilon_t \]

where \(\phi(L) = 1 - \phi_1 L - \dots - \phi_p L^p\) and \(\theta(L) = 1 + \theta_1 L + \dots + \theta_q L^q\)

  • What conditions ensure that \(y_t\) is stationary?

When Is an ARMA(p,q) Stationary?

  • If the roots of \(\phi(L)\) are outside the unit circle, then \(\phi(L)^{-1}\) is well defined
  • Inverting \(\phi(L)\) gives an MA(\(\infty\)):

\[ y_t = \frac{\mu}{1 - \phi_1 - \dots - \phi_p} + \underbrace{\phi(L)^{-1}\theta(L)}_{\equiv\, \psi(L)}\, \varepsilon_t \]

  • So \(\E[y_t] = \frac{\mu}{1 - \phi_1 - \dots - \phi_p}\)

Takeaway

Stationarity depends only on the roots of \(\phi(L)\), never on the roots of \(\theta(L)\)

What Autocovariances Do We Get?

  • For \(h > q\), the MA terms in \(y_t\) are uncorrelated with \(y_{t-h}\), so

\[ \gamma_h = \phi_1 \gamma_{h-1} + \dots + \phi_p \gamma_{h-p}, \qquad h > q \]

  • Roots of \(\phi(L)\) outside the unit circle \(\implies \gamma_h \to 0\) exponentially fast
  • For \(h \leq q\), things are way more complicated (Brockwell and Davis (1991), Section 3.3)
  • The last \(q\) shocks can create very complex dynamics without affecting stationarity

Takeaway

After lag \(q\) the decay is fast and exponential. The MA part never creates trouble for stationarity

Example: ARMA(1,1)

  • Consider an ARMA(1,1) process:

\[ y_t = \mu + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1}, \qquad |\phi_1| < 1 \]

  • We already know that \(\gamma_h = \phi_1 \gamma_{h-1}\) for \(h \geq 2\)
  • So we just need \(\gamma_0\) and \(\gamma_1\)
  • Two facts we will use, both from the MA(\(\infty\)) representation:

\[ \Cov(\varepsilon_t, y_t) = \sigma^2, \qquad \Cov(\varepsilon_{t-1}, y_t) = \phi_1 \sigma^2 + \theta_1 \sigma^2 \]

  • And \(\Cov(\varepsilon_t, y_{t-1}) = 0\), since \(y_{t-1}\) only depends on past shocks

Solving for \(\gamma_0\) and \(\gamma_1\)

Take covariances of both sides of the model with \(y_t\) and with \(y_{t-1}\): \[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_0 &= \phi_1 \gamma_1 + \Cov(\varepsilon_t, y_t) + \theta_1 \Cov(\varepsilon_{t-1}, y_t) = \phi_1 \gamma_1 + \sigma^2 + \theta_1 (\phi_1 + \theta_1) \sigma^2 \\ \gamma_1 &= \phi_1 \gamma_0 + \Cov(\varepsilon_t, y_{t-1}) + \theta_1 \Cov(\varepsilon_{t-1}, y_{t-1}) = \phi_1 \gamma_0 + \theta_1 \sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]

Plug the second equation into the first and solve the linear system: \[ \begin{aligned} \gamma_0 &= \frac{1 + \theta_1^2 + 2 \theta_1 \phi_1}{1 - \phi_1^2}\, \sigma^2 \\ \gamma_1 &= \frac{(1 + \theta_1 \phi_1)(\phi_1 + \theta_1)}{1 - \phi_1^2}\, \sigma^2 \\ \gamma_h &= \phi_1^{h-1} \gamma_1, \qquad h \geq 2 \end{aligned} \]

  • Wait a minute… what happens if \(\theta_1 = -\phi_1\)?

The Minimal Representation

  • In that case \(\gamma_h = 0\) for all \(h \geq 1\): \(y_t\) is white noise!!!
  • ARMA models can be overparameterized
  • Factor the lag polynomials using their roots:

\[ \phi(L) = (1 - r_1 L)(1 - r_2 L) \cdots (1 - r_p L), \qquad \theta(L) = (1 + s_1 L)(1 + s_2 L) \cdots (1 + s_q L) \]

  • A common factor cancels: same autocovariances with \(p-1\) lags of \(y_t\) and \(q-1\) lags of \(\varepsilon_t\)
  • A theoretical reason to prefer small \(p\) and \(q\) when estimating ARMA models
  • When we write “ARMA(p,q)”, we implicitly mean the minimal representation

Forecasting with ARMA Models

Forecasting: The Setup

  • ARMA models are extremely useful for forecasting
  • They are a simple way to capture complicated dynamics
  • Our job: compute conditional expectations of some process \(y_t\)
  • Our data: \(\{y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T\}\), a strip of one realized path with \(T\) observations
  • Exact forecasts depend on the infinite past of the process
  • We will use the available information to compute approximate forecasts

Forecasting with Infinite Data

  • Suppose \(y_t\) is a stationary ARMA and we have its MA(\(\infty\)) representation:

\[ y_t - \mu = \psi(L)\, \varepsilon_t = \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \psi_i \varepsilon_{t-i}, \qquad \psi_0 = 1, \quad \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} |\psi_i| < \infty \]

  • We want \(\E[y_{t+s} \mid \varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-1}, \dots]\). Split the shocks into future and past:

\[ y_{t+s} - \mu = \underbrace{\varepsilon_{t+s} + \psi_1 \varepsilon_{t+s-1} + \dots + \psi_{s-1} \varepsilon_{t+1}}_{\text{future: mean zero}} + \underbrace{\psi_s \varepsilon_t + \psi_{s+1} \varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots}_{\text{known at } t} \]

  • This implies

\[ \E[y_{t+s} \mid \varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-1}, \dots] = \mu + \sum_{i=s}^{\infty} \psi_i \varepsilon_{t+s-i} \]

The Annihilation Operator

  • A useful shorthand. Notice that

\[ \frac{\psi(L)}{L^s} = L^{-s} + \psi_1 L^{1-s} + \dots + \psi_{s-1} L^{-1} + \psi_s + \psi_{s+1} L + \psi_{s+2} L^2 + \dots \]

  • The annihilation operator \([\,\cdot\,]_+\) keeps only the nonnegative powers of \(L\):

\[ \left[\frac{\psi(L)}{L^s}\right]_+ \equiv \psi_s + \psi_{s+1} L + \psi_{s+2} L^2 + \dots \]

  • Now we can write

\[ \E[y_{t+s} \mid \varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-1}, \dots] = \mu + \left[\frac{\psi(L)}{L^s}\right]_+ \varepsilon_t \]

Wait, This Feels Like Cheating…

  • We never really observe the shocks \(\varepsilon_t\). How can this be useful?
  • Sometimes we can back them out from infinite data!
  • Back to the ARMA representation: \(\phi(L)(y_t - \mu) = \theta(L)\, \varepsilon_t\)
  • Roots of \(\phi(L)\) outside the unit circle \(\implies \phi(L)^{-1}\) is well defined
  • Roots of \(\theta(L)\) outside the unit circle \(\implies \theta(L)^{-1}\) is well defined as well!
  • In this case, the model is invertible:

\[ \theta(L)^{-1} \phi(L)\, (y_t - \mu) = \varepsilon_t \implies \varepsilon_t = \eta(L)\, (y_t - \mu) \]

where \(\eta(L) \equiv \theta(L)^{-1} \phi(L) = \psi(L)^{-1} = \eta_0 + \eta_1 L + \eta_2 L^2 + \dots\)

The General Formula

Wiener–Kolmogorov Prediction Formula

If \(y_t\) is a stationary and invertible ARMA\((p,q)\), i.e., the roots of both \(\phi(L)\) and \(\theta(L)\) lie outside the unit circle, then \[ \E[y_{t+s} \mid y_t, y_{t-1}, \dots] = \mu + \left[\frac{\psi(L)}{L^s}\right]_+ \eta(L)\, (y_t - \mu) \]

  • Conditioning on past \(y\)’s = conditioning on past \(\varepsilon\)’s: each is linear in the other
  • In practice, a computer does the algebra to get the coefficients
  • But it is really important to understand why a computer can do that!

Example: AR(1)

  • Assume that \((1 - \phi L)(y_t - \mu) = \varepsilon_t\) with \(|\phi| < 1\)
  • Then \(\psi(L) = (1 - \phi L)^{-1} = 1 + \phi L + \phi^2 L^2 + \dots\) and \(\eta(L) = 1 - \phi L\)
  • The annihilation operator gives

\[ \left[\frac{\psi(L)}{L^s}\right]_+ = \phi^s + \phi^{s+1} L + \phi^{s+2} L^2 + \dots = \phi^s \left(1 + \phi L + \phi^2 L^2 + \dots\right) = \frac{\phi^s}{1 - \phi L} \]

  • Multiplying by \(\eta(L) = 1 - \phi L\):

\[ \E[y_{t+s} \mid y_t, y_{t-1}, \dots] = \mu + \phi^s\, (y_t - \mu) \]

  • What happens when \(s \to \infty\)? What’s the intuition?

Example: MA(q)

  • Now assume \(y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q}\)
  • Then \(\psi(L) = \theta(L)\) and \(\eta(L) = \theta(L)^{-1}\)
  • If \(s > q\), the annihilation operator gives zero, so \(\E[y_{t+s} \mid y_t, y_{t-1}, \dots] = \mu\)
  • What’s the intuition for this result?
  • If \(s \leq q\):

\[ \left[\frac{\psi(L)}{L^s}\right]_+ = \theta_s + \theta_{s+1} L + \dots + \theta_q L^{q-s} \]

  • So the forecast is

\[ \E[y_{t+s} \mid y_t, y_{t-1}, \dots] = \mu + \frac{\theta_s + \theta_{s+1} L + \dots + \theta_q L^{q-s}}{\theta(L)}\, (y_t - \mu) \]

Forecasting with Finite Data

Forecasting with Finite Data

  • Bad news: so far we assumed infinite data. That is never the case…
  • Two main ways of dealing with this problem:
    1. Build an “approximate” forecast
    2. Use a method that explicitly accounts for the “missing data”
  • The most common method for (2) is the Kalman filter
  • No time to cover it, but super useful in Macro, Finance, DSGE estimation, etc.
  • Most statistical software uses (2) to construct forecasts
  • But learning (1) is instructive, and it gives the same results if \(T\) is large!

Approximating the Forecasts

  • Consider an ARMA\((p,q)\) process:

\[ y_t - \mu = \phi_1 (y_{t-1} - \mu) + \dots + \phi_p (y_{t-p} - \mu) + \varepsilon_t + \theta_1 \varepsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q \varepsilon_{t-q} \]

  • We have data \(\{y_1, \dots, y_T\}\) and want to forecast \(y_{T+h}\) for \(h > 0\)
  • The issue: the data are finite, and we never observe \(\varepsilon_t\)
  • Main trick: use the equation above to back out “estimated” shocks \(\hat\varepsilon_t\)
  • Presample convention: set \(\varepsilon_t = 0\) and \(y_t = \E[y_t] = \mu\) for \(t \leq 0\)

Backing Out the Shocks

With the presample convention (\(y_s - \mu = 0\) and \(\hat\varepsilon_s = 0\) for \(s \leq 0\)), recursively for \(t = 1, \dots, T\): \[ \hat\varepsilon_t = (y_t - \mu) - \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i (y_{t-i} - \mu) - \sum_{j=1}^{q} \theta_j \hat\varepsilon_{t-j} \]

The first few steps: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat\varepsilon_1 &= y_1 - \mu \\ \hat\varepsilon_2 &= (y_2 - \mu) - \phi_1 (y_1 - \mu) - \theta_1 \hat\varepsilon_1 \\ \hat\varepsilon_3 &= (y_3 - \mu) - \phi_1 (y_2 - \mu) - \phi_2 (y_1 - \mu) - \theta_1 \hat\varepsilon_2 - \theta_2 \hat\varepsilon_1 \end{aligned} \]

  • Essentially, \(\hat\varepsilon_t \equiv y_t - \hat y_{t|t-1}\): the one-step forecast error, given the presample convention

Iterating Forward

From here on, just iterate forward (Hamilton (1994), Equation 4.2.25): \[ \hat y_{T+h|T} - \mu = \sum_{i=1}^{p} \phi_i \left(\hat y_{T+h-i|T} - \mu\right) + \sum_{j=h}^{q} \theta_j \hat\varepsilon_{T+h-j} \]

  • Convention: \(\hat y_{\tau|T} = y_\tau\) for \(\tau \leq T\), and the second sum is empty when \(h > q\)
  • In practice: compute \(\hat y_{T+1|T}\), then \(\hat y_{T+2|T}\), then \(\hat y_{T+3|T}\), and so on
  • For \(h > q\) only the AR part is left, so the forecast approaches \(\mu\) exponentially fast
  • What’s the intuition for this result?
  • What ensures that the forecast does not explode exponentially fast, by the way?

// Forecast from origin T with the true shocks (infinite-data forecast), h = 1..24.
// The MA terms only matter for h <= q = 2; the variance uses the psi weights.
fcPath = {
  const [phi, T] = fc
  const {y, e} = fcSim
  const psi = [1, phi + fcTheta[0]]
  psi.push(phi * psi[1] + fcTheta[1])
  for (let j = 3; j < fcH; j++) psi.push(phi * psi[j - 1])
  const out = [{t: T, f: y[T - 1], sd: 0}]
  let prev = y[T - 1] - fcMu, v = 0
  for (let h = 1; h <= fcH; h++) {
    let f = phi * prev
    if (h === 1) f += fcTheta[0] * e[T - 1] + fcTheta[1] * e[T - 2]
    if (h === 2) f += fcTheta[1] * e[T - 1]
    v += psi[h - 1] ** 2
    out.push({t: T + h, f: fcMu + f, sd: Math.sqrt(v)})
    prev = f
  }
  return out
}

  return [Math.floor(lo) - 1, Math.ceil(hi) + 1]
}

The Wold Decomposition

Why So Much Attention to ARMA?

  • Why have we paid so much attention to ARMA models?
  • The Wold decomposition theorem is a powerful result
  • It essentially says that any stationary process has an MA(\(\infty\)) representation
  • By playing with \(p\) and \(q\), we can approximate that representation arbitrarily well
  • In a certain sense, ARMA models are “dense” in the space of stationary processes
  • Think of computers approximating real numbers with rationals all the time!

Wold’s Decomposition

Theorem (Wold’s Decomposition; Hamilton (1994), Section 4.8)

Let \(y_t\) be a zero-mean covariance-stationary process. Let \(\mathcal{P}_{t-m}[y_t]\) be the linear projection of \(y_t\) on \(\{y_{t-m}, y_{t-m-1}, \dots\}\), and let \(e_t \equiv y_t - \mathcal{P}_{t-1}[y_t]\) be the projection error. Then \(y_t\) has a unique representation \[ y_t = \mu_t + \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j e_{t-j} \] where \(e_t\) is white noise, \(\psi_0 = 1\), \(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty} \psi_j^2 < \infty\), \(\mu_t \equiv \lim_{m \to \infty} \mathcal{P}_{t-m}[y_t]\) is uncorrelated with \(e_{t-j}\) for all \(j\), and \(\mu_t\) is perfectly predictable from the distant past of \(y_t\)

Why Wold Matters

  • The only assumption is covariance stationarity: no linearity imposed anywhere
  • Yet every such process is a linear combination of its own one-step forecast errors
  • \(\psi_j\): how \(y_{t+j}\) responds to a shock today. Linear models are the natural language here
  • Catch: Wold has infinitely many \(\psi_j\), and we only have \(T\) observations
  • AR(1), \(\psi_j = 0.95^j\): dropping lags \(j > q\) loses under 1% of \(\Var(y_t)\) iff

\[ \frac{\sum_{j>q} 0.95^{2j}}{\sum_{j\geq 0} 0.95^{2j}} = 0.95^{2(q+1)} \leq 0.01 \iff q + 1 \geq \frac{\ln 0.01}{2 \ln 0.95} \approx 44.9 \iff q \geq 44 \]

  • As a ratio, \(\psi(L) = \frac{1}{1 - 0.95 L}\): one parameter, and the approximation is exact
  • A polynomial must cut off; a ratio generates long, smooth decay cheaply
  • ARMA methodology: approximate \(\psi(L)\) by \(\theta(L)/\phi(L)\) with small \(p\) and \(q\)
